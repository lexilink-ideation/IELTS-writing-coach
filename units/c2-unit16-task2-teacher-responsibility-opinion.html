C2 Task 2

Unit 16: Teachers and Students' Social Development — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree/Disagree) essay at its most sophisticated. Rather than simply agreeing or disagreeing, a Band 9 response weighs two competing views of where a child's social and moral formation properly belongs — the school as a socialising community versus the family as the first ethical ground — before landing on a qualified, partial-agreement position. Every paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practiced throughout the curriculum: an introduction with a background statement and a clear thesis statement, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence and restate your position, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + thesis statement (a qualified position). Paragraph 2 (For): the whole-person view, one named principle, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (Against): the family-first case, one named principle, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Middle path): a principle that resolves the tension + your reasoned position. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summary + confident final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • A partial-agreement thesis ("agree in part, disagree in part, for this specific reason") scores better than a flat yes/no — it shows the nuance examiners look for at Band 7+.
  • Name a principle (socialisation, family-first formation, subsidiarity) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, timeframes) beats breadth.
  • Keep the state-schooling critique analytical, never conspiratorial: say schools are structurally oriented toward useful conformity, not that they only ever indoctrinate — and avoid jargon like "ideological state apparatus" in the actual essay.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + state a partial-agreement thesis.
  • Body Paragraph 1 — the case FOR (the whole-person view: a school is itself a social community).
  • Body Paragraph 2 — the case AGAINST (moral formation belongs to the family; and schooling tends to produce "useful" citizens, not autonomous moral persons).
  • Body Paragraph 3 — a middle path (subsidiarity: the family leads, the school supports) + your reasoned position.
  • Conclusion — summarize + confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that you will weigh two competing views of where social and moral formation properly belongs — the school as a socialising community versus the family as the first ethical ground — before landing on a qualified position. Avoid a flat, one-sided thesis; IELTS examiners reward a nuanced stance in agree/disagree essays as much as a decisive one.

Example paraphrase: "It is sometimes claimed that teachers should be answerable not only for students' academic progress but also for their social and moral growth. This essay will examine why schools inevitably shape social behaviour, why the deeper responsibility for moral formation nonetheless rests with the family, and will argue that teachers should hold this responsibility only in a limited, supporting sense."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For: The Whole-Person View

There is a strong case that social development cannot be separated from schooling at all. A classroom is itself a small society in which children spend much of their waking day, and they learn cooperation, fairness, turn-taking and self-restraint simply by taking part in it. On this view, teachers shape social growth whether or not it appears in their job description, so the honest question is not whether they are responsible but how deliberately they should be.

Philosophical concept: The whole-person / socialisation view (Aristotle's idea that virtue is formed through habit and practice within a community, and John Dewey's argument that the school is itself a social environment) — intellectual and social development are two aspects of one process, not separable tasks.

Example: In Japanese schools, the daily practice of tokkatsu — where pupils clean their own classrooms and serve one another lunch — is a deliberate, timetabled way of teaching responsibility, cooperation and respect, showing that many education systems already treat social development as part of a teacher's remit rather than an optional extra.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case Against: The Family First, and the Limits of Institutions

Yet there are two reasons to resist making teachers primarily responsible for social and moral growth. First, the foundations of character — empathy, honesty, self-control — are laid in early childhood, at home, before formal schooling begins and through a bond far closer than any teacher of thirty pupils can offer. Second, and more troublingly, mass schooling has historically been designed less to produce free moral individuals than to produce orderly, economically useful citizens; entrusting a state institution with the moulding of values therefore carries real risks.

Philosophical concept: Two strands combine here. The family-first tradition (Locke, who ranked virtue and good conduct above book-learning; Hegel, for whom the family is the first sphere of ethical life) holds that moral formation is grounded in the home. The reproduction critique (Foucault on schools producing disciplined, governable subjects, and J. S. Mill's warning that state education tends to "mould people to be exactly like one another") holds that institutions are structurally oriented toward useful conformity rather than moral autonomy.

Example: Nineteenth-century Prussia built the first system of compulsory state schooling explicitly to produce disciplined, obedient and patriotic citizens and soldiers — a model later copied across Europe and beyond — illustrating that mass education was designed as much to serve the needs of the state as to cultivate each child's independent moral character.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — A Middle Path: Subsidiarity

The two positions can be reconciled through the principle of subsidiarity: a social task should be handled by the smallest unit competent to do it, with larger institutions supporting rather than replacing it. Applied here, primary responsibility for moral and social formation belongs to the family, which has both the depth of bond and the early years in which character is shaped; the school's proper role is to reinforce and extend those foundations — offering a wider arena in which to practise cooperation and civility — not to supplant the family as the source of values.

Example: Finland's widely studied KiVa anti-bullying programme reduced bullying in participating schools, yet it comes with explicit guides for parents — reflecting a design assumption that lasting change in children's social behaviour needs the home and the school pulling in the same direction, so schools are effective partners in social development but poor substitutes for the family.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between the school as a socialising community and the family as the ground of moral formation, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that teachers should indeed help develop students' social skills, but as partners supporting the family rather than as the parties chiefly responsible for shaping who a child becomes.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • The whole-person / socialisation view — social growth is inseparable from schooling (Aristotle's habituation; Dewey's school as a social environment).
  • Family as the first ethical ground — moral formation is rooted in the home through the depth and timing of the parent-child bond (Locke, Hegel).
  • The reproduction critique — mass schooling tends to produce useful, conforming citizens rather than autonomous moral persons (Foucault, J. S. Mill).
  • Subsidiarity — responsibility should sit with the smallest competent unit (the family), with schools supporting, not supplanting, it.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Some parents believe that teachers should be responsible for teaching students both intellectual and social development. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

It is sometimes claimed that teachers should be answerable not only for students' academic progress but also for their social and moral growth. In my view, this responsibility does belong to teachers, but only in a limited, supporting sense, once the school's role as a community and the family's role as the first ground of character are weighed.

Social development arguably cannot be separated from schooling at all. A classroom is itself a small society in which children spend much of their waking day, absorbing cooperation, fairness and self-restraint. In Japanese schools, for instance, the daily practice of tokkatsu — in which pupils clean their classrooms and serve one another lunch — is a deliberate, timetabled way of teaching responsibility and respect. Teachers therefore shape character whether or not it appears in their job description.

The deeper foundations of character, however, are laid at home before formal schooling begins, through bonds no teacher of thirty pupils can match. A more troubling concern is that mass schooling has historically been designed less to produce free moral individuals than orderly, useful citizens. Nineteenth-century Prussia, for example, built the first compulsory state education system explicitly to create disciplined, obedient subjects, which shows that entrusting an institution with the moulding of values carries real risks.

These positions can be reconciled through the principle of subsidiarity, which holds that a task should sit with the smallest competent unit, with larger bodies supporting rather than replacing it. Primary responsibility for moral formation therefore belongs to the family, while the school's role is to reinforce and extend those foundations. Finland's KiVa anti-bullying programme, for instance, reduced bullying yet came with explicit guides for parents, showing that lasting change needs home and school pulling together.

In conclusion, teachers unavoidably influence how children learn to live alongside others and should do so deliberately, but they are best seen as partners supporting the family rather than the parties chiefly responsible for shaping who a child becomes.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • answerable (for) — responsible for something and required to explain or justify it to others.
  • in a limited, supporting sense — only to a restricted degree, and in a helping role rather than a leading one; the opposite of full, primary responsibility.
  • once A and B are both weighed — only after carefully considering and comparing two opposing sides, so you can judge which is stronger before concluding; "weigh" here means "to judge the relative importance of," not physical weight.
  • self-restraint — the ability to control one's own impulses, emotions or behaviour.
  • timetabled — built into the official daily schedule; planned as a fixed part of the routine rather than left to chance.
  • remit (noun) — the area of activity or responsibility officially given to a person or organisation.
  • foundations of character — the earliest and most basic moral qualities on which a person's later behaviour is built.
  • moulding of values — the deliberate shaping of a person's beliefs about right and wrong.
  • orderly — well-behaved, disciplined and arranged according to rules.
  • subsidiarity — the principle that a task should be handled by the smallest or most local unit able to do it, with larger bodies stepping in only to support.
  • reinforce and extend — to strengthen something that already exists and then build further on it.
  • supplant — to take the place of something and replace it entirely.
  • pulling in the same direction — working together toward the same goal rather than against one another.
  • substitute (for) (noun) — a person or thing used in place of another; here, something that cannot fully replace the original.
  • unavoidably — in a way that cannot be prevented or escaped.
  • chiefly responsible — mainly or primarily responsible; carrying the largest share of the duty.
