C2 Task 2

Unit 15: Protecting Only "Useful" Animals — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree/Disagree) essay at its most sophisticated. Rather than simply agreeing or disagreeing, a Band 9 response weighs two competing accounts of moral value — instrumental (usefulness-based) versus intrinsic — before landing on a qualified position. Every paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practiced throughout the curriculum: an introduction with a background statement and a clear thesis statement, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence and restate your position, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + thesis statement (a qualified position). Paragraph 2 (For): anthropocentric case, one named principle, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (Against): intrinsic-value case, one named principle, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Middle path): a principle that resolves the tension + your reasoned position. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summary + confident final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • A qualified-disagreement thesis ("disagree, except where resource constraints force practical triage") scores better than a flat yes/no — it shows the nuance examiners look for at Band 7+.
  • Name a principle (anthropocentrism, intrinsic value, ecological interdependence) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, dates, figures) beats breadth.
  • Avoid philosophy jargon like "anthropocentrism" or "speciesism" in the actual essay — translate the idea into plain, natural English.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + state a qualified-disagreement thesis.
  • Body Paragraph 1 — the case FOR (anthropocentrism: value is defined relative to human need).
  • Body Paragraph 2 — the case AGAINST (intrinsic value & anti-speciesism: worth is not conditional on utility).
  • Body Paragraph 3 — a middle path (ecocentrism / interdependence) + your reasoned position.
  • Conclusion — summarize + confident final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that you will weigh two competing accounts of moral value — instrumental (usefulness-based) versus intrinsic — before landing on a qualified position. A flat, one-sided thesis is weaker than one that acknowledges why the utility view has appeal before rejecting it.

Example paraphrase: "It is sometimes argued that conservation efforts should be reserved for species that directly serve human interests, whether economic, agricultural or medical. This essay will examine the practical case for this view, the ethical case against it, and will argue that usefulness is ultimately too narrow — and too short-sighted — a standard for deciding which species deserve protection."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For: Anthropocentrism

An anthropocentric view holds that moral consideration flows outward from human interests: animals matter because, and to the extent that, they serve us — as food sources, pollinators, medical research subjects, or tourist attractions. Given finite conservation budgets, prioritizing species with clear human benefit can seem like responsible triage rather than callousness.

Philosophical concept: Anthropocentrism, with roots in Kant's view that humans have only "indirect duties" toward animals (cruelty is wrong because it coarsens human character, not because animals have worth in themselves): value is assigned relative to human purposes.

Example: A widely cited study on global conservation funding found that "flagship" or economically valuable species — pandas, tigers, elephants, and pollinators such as honeybees — receive a hugely disproportionate share of budgets and donor attention, while less charismatic but equally endangered groups, such as amphibians and freshwater mollusks, remain critically underfunded despite amphibians being the most threatened vertebrate class on Earth.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case Against: Intrinsic Value and Anti-Speciesism

Philosopher Peter Singer argues that the morally relevant criterion is sentience — the capacity to suffer — not usefulness to another species. Judging an animal's worth by its utility to humans, Singer contends, is a form of "speciesism": an arbitrary discrimination structurally similar to judging a person's worth by race or sex, since usefulness to us has nothing to do with the animal's own capacity to suffer or flourish. Tom Regan goes further, arguing that any "subject-of-a-life" — a being with its own beliefs, memory and interests — has inherent value that cannot be traded off for human convenience.

Philosophical concept: Intrinsic value and the anti-speciesism critique (Peter Singer, Tom Regan): worth exists independently of usefulness to another species.

Example: The baiji, or Yangtze river dolphin, was declared functionally extinct in 2006 after decades of habitat loss and industrial development — a species with no major economic use to humans. Its disappearance is widely cited in conservation ethics as an irreversible loss of a unique evolutionary lineage, illustrating that value need not be tied to utility to be real.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — A Middle Path: Ecocentrism and Ecological Interdependence

Aldo Leopold's "land ethic" and later deep ecology (Arne Naess) argue that the proper unit of moral concern is the whole biotic community, not any single species judged in isolation. This view also does useful practical work: it shows that the utility standard is self-undermining, since a species dismissed as "useless" today may prove ecologically essential once removed — usefulness is often invisible until it is gone.

Example: When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 after a decades-long absence, researchers documented a "trophic cascade": elk populations thinned and changed grazing behavior, riverside vegetation recovered, and this in turn stabilized riverbanks and increased biodiversity. Wolves had been eliminated in the 1920s as a species with no use to humans and, indeed, a threat to livestock — yet their absence measurably degraded the ecosystem.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between anthropocentric utility and intrinsic/ecocentric value, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that while limited conservation resources may require practical prioritization, usefulness to humans should never be the defining criterion for which species deserve protection, since it is both ethically arbitrary and ecologically short-sighted.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Anthropocentrism — value is defined relative to human interests and needs.
  • Intrinsic value — worth exists in a being independent of its usefulness to others.
  • Speciesism (Peter Singer) — privileging one species' interests over another's based on an irrelevant trait, comparable to racism or sexism.
  • Animal rights (Tom Regan) — "subjects-of-a-life" have inherent value and rights that cannot be overridden by convenience.
  • Ecocentrism / the land ethic (Aldo Leopold) — moral concern extends to the whole biotic community, not isolated species.
  • Value pluralism — the disagreement may reflect differing views of what conservation is FOR (efficient resource use vs. moral/ecological obligation), not just a factual dispute.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Many animal species are becoming endangered. Some people believe that we should only protect animals that are useful to humans. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

It is sometimes suggested that conservation efforts should be reserved for species that directly benefit humans, whether through food, medicine or tourism. In my view, this claim is largely unconvincing, since usefulness is too narrow and too short-sighted a standard for deciding which species deserve protection, even though limited resources may occasionally require practical prioritisation.

There is admittedly a pragmatic case for favouring species with clear human value. Given finite conservation budgets, channelling funding toward species that support agriculture, medicine or tourism can resemble responsible triage rather than indifference. This logic partly explains why flagship species such as pandas, tigers and honeybees receive a hugely disproportionate share of global conservation funding, while equally endangered but less charismatic groups, such as amphibians, remain critically underfunded despite being the most threatened vertebrate class on Earth.

Nevertheless, an animal's worth need not depend on its usefulness to another species. What matters morally, on this view, is an animal's own capacity to suffer or flourish, not what it can offer humans; judging a creature by its usefulness to us is no more defensible than judging a person by some arbitrary trait unrelated to their inherent worth. The baiji, or Yangtze river dolphin, had no significant economic value to humans, yet its extinction in 2006 is widely regarded as an irreversible loss, demonstrating that value need not be tied to utility to be real.

A more balanced position recognises that species do not exist in isolation but within an interdependent ecological web whose usefulness is often invisible until it is gone. When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995, decades after being eliminated as a species with no perceived use to humans, elk grazing patterns shifted, riverside vegetation recovered, and biodiversity increased, showing that their absence had quietly degraded the whole ecosystem.

In conclusion, while scarce resources may justify some pragmatic prioritisation, usefulness to humans should never be the defining standard for protection, since it is both ethically arbitrary and ecologically short-sighted.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • unconvincing — not persuasive; failing to convince someone that a claim is correct.
  • short-sighted — lacking careful consideration of future consequences; focused only on immediate, present concerns.
  • pragmatic — dealing with things sensibly and realistically, based on practical considerations rather than ideals.
  • triage — the practice of ranking priorities according to urgency or importance when resources are limited.
  • channelling funding — directing money or resources toward a particular purpose.
  • hugely disproportionate — far too large or too small in relation to something else; seriously out of proportion.
  • flagship species — a well-known species chosen to represent a broader conservation cause because it attracts public attention.
  • charismatic (of a species) — naturally appealing to people, and therefore more likely to attract funding and attention.
  • vertebrate class — a major biological group of animals with backbones, such as mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians or fish.
  • capacity to suffer or flourish — the ability to experience pain or well-being; often used as the basis for moral consideration.
  • arbitrary — based on random choice or personal whim rather than any reason or system; without logical justification.
  • inherent worth — value that belongs to something in itself, not because of what it can do for someone else.
  • irreversible — impossible to change back to a previous state.
  • interdependent ecological web — a network of living things that all rely on one another to function.
  • invisible until it is gone — not noticeable or appreciated until it disappears or is lost.
  • quietly degraded — gradually made worse or damaged in a way that is not immediately obvious.
