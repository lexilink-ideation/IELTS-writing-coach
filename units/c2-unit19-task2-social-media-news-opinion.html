C2 Task 2

Unit 19: Social Media News and the Focus on Problems — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree/Disagree) essay at its most sophisticated. Rather than flatly agreeing or disagreeing, a Band 9 response weighs why social media news skews toward crises and emergencies — the pull of human attention toward threat and the platform incentives that profit from it — against the value that covering problems still has, before landing on a qualified, largely-agree position. Every paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practiced throughout the curriculum: an introduction with a background statement and a clear thesis statement, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence and restate your position, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + thesis statement (a qualified, largely-agree position). Paragraph 2 (Why it happens): negativity bias + the attention economy, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (The cost): good news is invisible and audiences misjudge reality, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (The qualification): covering problems has civic value and good news is not absent + your reasoned position. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summary + confident final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • A partial-agreement thesis ("I largely agree, because the attention economy rewards alarm — but this reflects human psychology and has some value") scores better than a flat yes/no — it shows the nuance examiners look for at Band 7+.
  • Name a principle (negativity bias, the attention economy, mean-world syndrome, the watchdog press) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, timeframes) beats breadth.
  • Keep the critique analytical, never conspiratorial: say platforms are structurally oriented toward capturing attention, not that they deliberately deceive — and tie every point back to the exact question of balance.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + state a partial-agreement thesis.
  • Body Paragraph 1 — WHY it happens: negativity draws attention, and the attention economy rewards it.
  • Body Paragraph 2 — WHY good news is invisible + the COST: a distorted, fearful picture of reality.
  • Body Paragraph 3 — the qualification: covering problems has real value, and positive news is not entirely absent + your reasoned position.
  • Conclusion — summarize + confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal a nuanced stance rather than a flat yes or no: agree that social media news leans heavily toward problems and emergencies, but explain that this reflects human psychology and platform incentives as much as editorial choice, and that the focus is not entirely without value. IELTS examiners reward a qualified position in agree/disagree essays as much as a decisive one, so name the direction of your argument in the introduction.

Example paraphrase: "It is often claimed that the news shared on social media dwells on crises and misfortune while overlooking good news. This essay will largely agree, arguing that platforms amplify alarming stories because human attention is drawn to threat and because engagement is profitable, and that this distorts how safe people believe the world to be — while acknowledging that reporting problems also serves a necessary public purpose, so the tendency is real but not wholly a fault."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — Why It Happens: Attention Is Drawn to Threat, and the Platform Profits From It

There is strong reason to agree that social media news skews negative, and the cause lies in two forces working together. First, people are simply more alert to danger than to reassurance — a bad headline stops the scroll in a way a good one does not. Second, social media platforms are built to maximise engagement, and fear, outrage and emergency generate more clicks, shares and comments than steady, undramatic good news. The negativity is therefore not a neutral accident but the predictable result of an audience wired for threat meeting an algorithm rewarded for capturing attention.

Philosophical concept: Two ideas combine. The negativity bias — anticipated by the Stoics and Epicurus, who observed that fear and imagined threats disturb us far more than reality warrants — explains why alarming news commands disproportionate attention. Alongside it, the attention economy (Herbert Simon's insight that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention") explains the incentive: when attention is the scarce resource being sold, content that provokes strong emotion wins the competition for it.

Example: During major events such as the COVID-19 pandemic, alarming case counts, death tolls and warnings dominated social feeds and spread far faster than stories of recoveries, vaccine progress or community support — a clear case of threat-driven attention and engagement incentives pushing emergency coverage to the top.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — Why Good News Is Invisible, and What It Costs Us

The tendency is reinforced by what "news" is taken to mean, and it carries a real cost. Positive developments — falling poverty, rising literacy, gradual gains in public health — tend to be slow, cumulative and undramatic, so they never become a single "event" that can be posted on a given day. Peace and normalcy are quiet by nature, while a disaster is sudden and vivid. The consequence is that heavy consumers of social media come to believe the world is far more dangerous and worse-off than the evidence shows, breeding anxiety and cynicism.

Philosophical concept: Two strands sharpen the point. An epistemological observation about newsworthiness — captured in the newsroom maxim "if it bleeds, it leads" — holds that news selects for the unusual and the sudden, which structurally filters out slow, positive change. The consequence is described by cultivation theory (George Gerbner's "mean world syndrome"): sustained exposure to alarming content makes audiences perceive the world as more threatening than it is. Hans Rosling's "negativity instinct" in Factfulness makes the same case with data.

Example: Surveys inspired by Rosling's work repeatedly find that the public underestimates progress on extreme poverty, child mortality and access to education — a gap between perception and reality that a problem-focused, emergency-driven feed both reflects and deepens.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — The Qualification: Covering Problems Has Value, and Good News Is Not Absent

It would be one-sided, however, to treat this focus as purely a failing. There is a legitimate reason emergencies dominate: problems are actionable. Reporting a crisis warns people of genuine danger, holds the powerful to account and can mobilise help and reform — a feed of only cheerful news would be complacent, even negligent. Moreover, social media is not monolithic: viral acts of kindness, "solutions journalism" and dedicated good-news accounts do circulate, so the statement describes a strong tendency rather than an absolute rule. A fair position, then, is to agree that the balance tilts heavily toward the negative, while recognising that some of this is both natural and necessary.

Philosophical concept: This rests on the democratic "watchdog" conception of the press: the value of journalism lies partly in exposing what is wrong so that citizens can respond and authorities can be held accountable. On this view, attention to problems is not mere pessimism but a civic function — the difficulty is one of proportion and balance, not of covering problems at all.

Example: The same platforms that spread emergency news have also driven positive movements and fundraising — from viral disaster-relief campaigns to good-news pages with millions of followers — showing that while problems dominate, social media can and does carry positive developments when they are given the chance to spread.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between social media's heavy focus on problems and emergencies and the relative invisibility of positive developments, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that you largely agree, because human attention is drawn to threat and platforms profit from amplifying it, which leaves audiences with an overly bleak view of the world; but that reporting problems also serves a necessary public purpose and positive stories are not wholly absent, so the real issue is one of balance rather than a simple fault.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Negativity bias — people attend to threat more than reassurance, so alarming news commands disproportionate attention (Stoics and Epicurus on the outsized power of fear).
  • The attention economy — where attention is the scarce resource being sold, emotionally provocative content wins, favouring fear and outrage (Herbert Simon).
  • Newsworthiness / "if it bleeds, it leads" — news selects for the sudden and unusual, structurally filtering out slow, positive change.
  • Mean world syndrome — heavy exposure to alarming content makes people believe the world is more dangerous than it is (George Gerbner's cultivation theory; Hans Rosling's negativity instinct).
  • The watchdog press — covering problems is a civic function: it warns the public and holds power to account, so the issue is proportion, not coverage itself.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: News reports on social media tend to focus on problems and emergencies rather than positive developments. To what extent do you agree or disagree with this statement?

It is often claimed that the news circulating on social media dwells on crises and misfortune while overlooking positive developments. In my view, this is largely correct, though the tendency reflects human psychology and platform incentives as much as editorial bias, and it is not entirely without value.

Alarming news dominates because attention is drawn to threat and platforms profit from capturing it. People are more alert to danger than to reassurance — a frightening headline stops the scroll in a way a hopeful one does not — and algorithms built to maximise engagement reward the fear and outrage that follow. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, rising case counts and death tolls spread far faster than stories of recovery, illustrating how the negativity bias and the attention economy push emergencies to the top of every feed.

This imbalance carries a real cost, because positive change is usually slow and undramatic. Falling poverty or rising literacy never becomes a single postable "event," so heavy users come to believe the world is more dangerous than it is. Surveys inspired by Hans Rosling's Factfulness find that the public underestimates global progress on poverty and child mortality — a "mean world" distortion that a problem-focused feed both mirrors and deepens.

It would be one-sided, however, to treat this focus as purely a failing. Covering problems is a civic function: it warns people of genuine danger and holds the powerful to account, and viral fundraising shows that good news does circulate when given the chance. The difficulty is one of proportion, not of coverage itself.

In conclusion, I largely agree that social media news skews toward problems and emergencies, but this reflects natural instincts and legitimate purposes as much as fault, leaving balance as the real concern.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • to dwell on (something) — to keep giving attention to something, especially something negative, instead of moving past it.
  • largely correct — true for the most part, though not completely or without exception; a qualified way of agreeing.
  • editorial bias — a deliberate slant in what a news outlet chooses to report or how it frames it; here contrasted with unconscious psychological and platform forces.
  • drawn to threat — naturally pulled toward noticing danger; describes the human tendency to pay more attention to what is alarming.
  • stops the scroll — makes a user pause instead of continuing to swipe past content on a feed; a vivid way of describing what grabs attention online.
  • the negativity bias — the psychological tendency to notice, remember and react to bad information more strongly than to good.
  • the attention economy — the idea that human attention is a scarce, valuable resource that platforms compete to capture and sell.
  • undramatic — lacking excitement or a sudden striking quality; here used of slow, positive change that never becomes "news."
  • postable "event" — a single, discrete happening dramatic enough to be shared on a given day; slow progress rarely qualifies.
  • mean world (distortion) — the false impression, created by heavy exposure to alarming content, that the world is more dangerous than it really is.
  • to mirror and deepen — to both reflect an existing distortion and make it worse.
  • one-sided — considering only one point of view and ignoring the other; an unbalanced judgement.
  • a civic function — a role that serves the public good and the health of a democracy.
  • to hold the powerful to account — to force those in power to explain and answer for their actions.
  • a matter of proportion — a question of relative amount or balance rather than of something being wholly right or wrong.
  • to skew toward — to lean or be distorted in a particular direction rather than being balanced.
