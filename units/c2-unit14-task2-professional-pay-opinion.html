C2 Task 2

Unit 14: Professional Pay vs. Sports & Entertainment — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree/Disagree) essay at its most sophisticated. Rather than simply agreeing or disagreeing, a Band 9 response weighs two competing principles of distributive justice — moral desert versus market value — before landing on a qualified, partial-agreement position. Every paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practiced throughout the curriculum: an introduction with a background statement and a clear thesis statement, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence and restate your position, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + thesis statement (a qualified position). Paragraph 2 (For): moral case, one named principle, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (Against): market case, one named principle, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Middle path): a fairness principle that resolves the tension + your reasoned position. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summary + confident final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • A partial-agreement thesis ("agree in part, disagree in part, for this specific reason") scores better than a flat yes/no — it shows the nuance examiners look for at Band 7+.
  • Name a principle (desert, market efficiency, Rawlsian fairness) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, figures, timeframes) beats breadth.
  • Avoid philosophy jargon like "deontological" or "utilitarian calculus" in the actual essay — translate the idea into plain, natural English.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + state a partial-agreement thesis.
  • Body Paragraph 1 — the case FOR (desert theory: pay should track moral/social value).
  • Body Paragraph 2 — the case AGAINST (market logic: pay tracks scarcity, not virtue).
  • Body Paragraph 3 — a middle path (Rawls's difference principle) + your reasoned position.
  • Conclusion — summarize + confident final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that you will weigh two competing principles of distributive justice — moral desert versus market value — before landing on a qualified position. Avoid a flat, one-sided thesis; IELTS examiners reward a nuanced stance in agree/disagree essays as much as a decisive one.

Example paraphrase: "It is sometimes argued that those in essential public-service roles — medicine, nursing and education — merit higher salaries than athletes and entertainers. This essay will examine the moral case for this view, the economic case against it, and will argue that the claim is justified only in a limited, conditional sense."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For: Desert Theory

Desert theory holds that reward should track the moral weight of what a person contributes, not simply what the market will pay for it. Doctors, nurses and teachers produce goods almost everyone treats as intrinsically necessary — health, safety, and an educated citizenry — while entertainment produces goods that, however enjoyable, are comparatively optional and substitutable.

Philosophical concept: Desert-based justice (associated with Aristotle's idea of distribution "according to merit," and revived in modern debates about just deserts) — rewards should be proportional to moral or social contribution.

Example: During the COVID-19 pandemic, many countries publicly hailed nurses and doctors as "key workers," yet frontline NHS nurses in the UK earned roughly £30,000-£35,000 a year, while top Premier League footballers earned comparable sums in a single week — a gap critics cited as evidence that pay and social value had become disconnected.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case Against: Market and Utilitarian Logic

Under a market system, wages track scarcity and demand, not moral worth. A footballer or pop star can command enormous earnings because a scarce, hard-to-replicate skill is being bid for by millions of paying fans simultaneously, whereas clinical or teaching skill — while harder-won in years of training — is comparatively more abundant relative to demand, and often further constrained by government budgets rather than open markets.

Philosophical concept: Sherwin Rosen's "economics of superstars" and utilitarian welfare reasoning — if wages already channel talent to where it creates the most aggregate satisfaction, redistributing pay by moral judgment rather than demand could reduce overall efficiency and welfare.

Example: Global streaming and broadcasting let a single performer's skill reach hundreds of millions of people at near-zero marginal cost, which is why a handful of entertainers and athletes (e.g., top football transfer fees exceeding £100 million) command valuations no salary board for nurses or teachers, operating on public budgets, could realistically match.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — A Middle Path: Rawls's Difference Principle

John Rawls argued that inequalities in reward are just only if they ultimately benefit the least advantaged members of society. Applied here: higher pay for doctors, nurses and teachers is justified to the extent it is necessary to attract enough talented people into professions that keep the worst-off healthy and educated — not simply because the work feels more "virtuous." Entertainment inequality, meanwhile, is tolerable so long as it does not crowd out investment in these essential services.

Example: Finland and Singapore both deliberately raised teachers' relative pay and status to attract top graduates into the profession, a policy credited with contributing to strong national education outcomes — an application of Rawlsian reasoning rather than a moral claim that teachers simply "deserve" more than entertainers.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between desert and market logic, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that essential-service pay should rise where it is demonstrably too low to retain talent, without asserting that market-driven entertainment earnings are inherently unjust.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Desert theory — reward should match moral/social contribution.
  • Market/utilitarian logic — reward should match scarcity and demand; efficient allocation of talent maximizes welfare.
  • Rawls's difference principle — inequality is just only if it benefits the least advantaged.
  • Value pluralism — the disagreement may reflect differing views of what pay is FOR (efficient coordination vs. moral signaling), not just a factual dispute.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Professional workers such as doctors, nurses and teachers should be paid more than people in sports and entertainment. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

It is sometimes argued that people in essential public-service roles, such as medicine, nursing and education, merit higher salaries than athletes and entertainers. In my view, this claim is justified, but only in a limited, conditional sense, once both the moral case for it and the economic case against it are weighed.

Reward arguably should track the moral weight of a person's contribution, not merely what the market will pay. Doctors, nurses and teachers provide goods almost everyone regards as essential, namely health, safety and an educated population, whereas entertainment, however enjoyable, remains comparatively optional. This disconnect became visible during the COVID-19 pandemic, when many countries praised nurses as "key workers," yet frontline NHS nurses in the UK earned roughly £30,000 to £35,000 a year, a sum top Premier League footballers could earn in a single week. For this reason, it is easy to sympathize with the view that essential professions are undervalued.

Wages in a market economy, however, track scarcity and demand rather than moral worth, and this logic is not unreasonable either. A footballer or pop star can command huge earnings because a rare, hard-to-replicate skill is bid for by millions of fans simultaneously through global streaming, at almost no extra cost per viewer. Clinical and teaching skill, though harder-won, is comparatively more abundant and constrained by public budgets, which is why transfer fees exceeding £100 million are unimaginable for a public salary board. Entertainment pay therefore reflects how differently the two sectors are financed, not simple injustice.

A more balanced position, close to the philosopher John Rawls's idea that inequality is fair only if it improves the situation of society's most vulnerable members, resolves this tension. Higher pay for doctors, nurses and teachers is warranted where necessary to attract enough talented people into professions that keep the vulnerable healthy and educated, not simply because the work feels more virtuous. Finland and Singapore, for instance, raised teachers' relative pay and status to draw top graduates, a policy credited with strong education outcomes.

In conclusion, professional workers' pay does lag behind that of top entertainers, but this gap is only unjust where it is too low to retain the talent society depends on, not because market-driven entertainment earnings are inherently wrong.
