C2 Task 2

Unit 24: Should People Be Required to Train Before Becoming Parents? — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Agree / Disagree, Defined Partial Agreement, Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree / Disagree) essay, and the task type changes what the examiner is looking for. "To what extent do you agree or disagree" is not the same as "discuss both views." You are not obliged to give each side an equal paragraph; you are obliged to take a position and defend it consistently from the first paragraph to the last. The phrase "to what extent" is an explicit invitation to agree partially — but a partial agreement must be a defined position, not a vague one. "I somewhat agree" scores nothing; "I accept the aim and reject the mechanism" is a thesis an entire essay can be built on.

A second warning specific to this question: the prompt never says whether the training would be compulsory. "Should receive professional training" could mean that courses ought to be widely available and expected, or that nobody should be permitted to have a child without a certificate. These are completely different proposals facing completely different objections, and an essay that slides between them will contradict itself. Define the term in your introduction — this costs one clause and is itself a Band 8 move, because clarifying an ambiguous key term is exactly the judgement examiners reward.

A third warning: this is a question about a policy, not a question about parenting. "Raising a child is difficult and important" is true, universally agreed, and worth no marks. Every point must attach to the proposal itself — who would design the course, who would administer the test, and what would happen to a person who failed it. A moving essay about how demanding parenthood is has not answered the question that was asked.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + define what version of "training" you are addressing + state your position. Paragraph 2 (The case for): society already assesses parental competence whenever a child arrives by any route other than birth, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (The case against): competence cannot be measured in advance, no curriculum is neutral, and a gate must do something with those who fail it, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Your position): separate provision from permission, one specific example. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): restate the tension + confident, qualified final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • Make the partial agreement specific. Say precisely which part of the proposal you accept and which part you reject, and hold that line in every paragraph.
  • Give the opposing case a full, sympathetic paragraph. Even in an opinion essay, presenting the view you reject at its strongest is what makes your own position look reasoned rather than reflexive.
  • Name a principle in plain English (provision versus permission, the enforcement problem, the adoption asymmetry) — this makes the reasoning visible and supports both coherence and task response.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, figures) beats breadth.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 270-330 words total. Note that a genuine Band 9 response tends to sit at the upper end of that range: making your position explicit in every paragraph and explaining each causal step properly simply costs words, and Task 2 has a 250-word minimum with no upper limit.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question, define what "training" is being proposed, and state your position.
  • Body Paragraph 1 (The case for) — society already assesses parental competence whenever a child arrives by any route other than birth, and structured preparation demonstrably improves outcomes.
  • Body Paragraph 2 (The case against) — parental competence cannot be measured before the fact, any curriculum encodes a contested cultural norm, and nobody can say what should happen to those who fail.
  • Body Paragraph 3 (Your position) — separate provision from permission, and notice that the benefits belong to the first while the dangers belong to the second.
  • Conclusion — restate the tension + a confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then say immediately which version of the proposal you are addressing — availability or permission — and where you stand. Your opinion belongs here, not saved as a surprise for the conclusion.

Example paraphrase: "In most countries a person must pass an examination to drive a car, dispense medicine or foster a child for a single weekend, yet may raise a child of their own with no preparation whatsoever. Some regard this as an obvious gap in the way societies protect the vulnerable, while others regard any attempt to close it as an intrusion into the most private decision a person can make. This essay argues that the impulse behind the proposal is sound but the mechanism usually imagined for it is not: preparation for parenthood should be universally provided, actively encouraged and socially expected, yet it should never become a condition of permission, because everything valuable in the idea survives without a gate and almost everything dangerous in it depends on one."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For: We Already Accept the Principle, We Simply Apply It Selectively

The strongest version of the pro-training argument does not begin with how hard parenting is. It begins with an inconsistency. Societies license activities that are dangerous to others and require competence — driving, medicine, food handling, keeping certain animals — and they already assess parental capacity with great rigour whenever a child arrives by any route other than birth. Prospective adopters and foster carers are trained, interviewed, medically examined, referenced and formally approved. The principle that parental competence can be assessed, and that a child's interests justify assessing it, is therefore not a radical proposal at all; it is already law in most of the world. What has never been justified is the exemption. The fact that a woman gave birth to a particular child is a biological fact about how the child arrived, not an argument about how well she will raise him.

Philosophical concept: Mill's harm principle is usually cited against state interference, but here it cuts the other way: a child is a third party who never consented to the arrangement, so having a child is not self-regarding conduct and liberty arguments do not automatically shelter it. Behind that sits Locke's account of parental authority as derived from parental duty — the parent has power over the child because the parent owes the child something, not because the child is property. Onora O'Neill sharpens this into the modern formulation: parents' rights are derivative of children's rights, not independent of them. If parenthood is a trust rather than a possession, attaching conditions to the trustee is an ordinary feature of trusteeship, not tyranny.

Example: In England, a person wishing to adopt enters a two-stage statutory assessment: Stage 1, normally completed within two months, covers registration, references, medical checks and preparation training; Stage 2, which should take about four months, is an in-depth home study producing a Prospective Adopter's Report, which goes to an adoption panel, whose recommendation goes to the agency's decision-maker. Six months of assessment to raise a child who already exists; nothing at all to create one. Whatever one concludes, that asymmetry demands an explanation, and "it is natural" is not one, since almost everything a modern state regulates was once left to nature.

Developing the point — preparation demonstrably changes outcomes. The argument is not merely logical; there is evidence that structured guidance for new parents works. Extending the paragraph in this direction shows development rather than repetition, and it moves the case from a point about consistency to a point about results.

Example: The Nurse-Family Partnership began as a randomised controlled trial in Elmira, New York, in 1977, enrolling 400 women expecting a first child; nurses visited them from pregnancy until the child's second birthday. At the fifteen-year follow-up, published in the Journal of the American Medical Association in 1997, the visited families had 48 per cent fewer state-verified reports of child abuse and neglect than the control group — and this despite the fact that having a nurse in the house should, if anything, have increased detection.

State the limits, because doing so earns credit for judgement. The trial was small, ran in one rural area, and targeted first-time mothers on low incomes, so the results cannot simply be assumed to hold for the whole population; programmes of this kind have also proved harder to replicate at national scale than at trial scale. And note carefully what the evidence actually supports — every participant volunteered. The trial shows that help works. It does not show that screening works, and the distinction will matter in your final paragraph.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case Against: What Exactly Would Be Measured, and What Would Be Done With the Failures?

The opposing view raises two objections, one about knowledge and one about power. The first is that a course can transmit information — infant nutrition, safe sleep, developmental stages — but the qualities that actually determine how a child turns out are not informational. Patience at four in the morning, consistency maintained across eighteen years, the capacity to stay warm towards a child who is being deliberately unpleasant: none of these can be examined in advance, because none of them exists yet in the person being examined. A test would therefore certify the articulate rather than the capable, and those are not the same population. The second objection is that a curriculum is never neutral. Whether children should be smacked, whether they should sleep in their parents' bed, how much independence a seven-year-old should have, how much academic pressure is care and how much is cruelty — these are contested across cultures and classes, and there is no vantage point from which a state can settle them. A licence would therefore certify one culture's parenting and pathologise the rest, and it would fall hardest on the poor, the migrant and the less literate, who are already the most heavily surveilled and who would gain most from actual help.

Philosophical concept: Aristotle's phronesis, or practical wisdom, is the key idea: it is acquired through habituation in the situation, not through instruction before it, which is why you cannot examine somebody in year three of parenthood before year one has begun. Goodhart's law explains the second failure — once a measure becomes a target it ceases to measure what it measured, so candidates would prepare for the test rather than for the child. Winnicott's "good enough" parent supplies the standard a licensing regime would inevitably lose: the threshold that matters is adequacy, not excellence, and any examined threshold tends to ratchet upwards over time.

Example: The historical record here is not hypothetical, and the most useful example is not a colonial one, because a colonial example is too easily dismissed as belonging to a barbaric past. Sweden — a progressive Nordic welfare democracy — sterilised roughly 63,000 people between 1935 and 1976 under a programme whose stated purposes included preventing children from being raised by the unfit. Those selected were disproportionately poor, single mothers with several children, Roma, and people classed as "deviant"; a quarter of the recorded consents were obtained as a condition of release from an institution. Australia's Bringing Them Home report, tabled in 1997, documented the removal of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children from their families on the stated ground of saving them from neglect. A parenting course is obviously not a sterilisation programme. But the criterion is the same criterion — official judgement of who is fit to raise a child — and the record of what states have done once granted that power is the single strongest reason for caution.

Developing the point — the enforcement problem. The decisive objection is rarely stated but is very simple, and naming it is worth a great deal: a gate must do something with the people who fail it. There are only three possibilities. It can prevent them from conceiving, which cannot be achieved without coercion of the kind described above. It can remove the child at birth, which is the historical catastrophe rather than a policy. Or it can do nothing — in which case the licence is not a licence at all but a leaflet, and the entire proposal collapses into the far milder one of simply offering training. A licence with no penalty is not a licence; a licence with a penalty is a horror. That dilemma, and not any general point about privacy, is what defeats the compulsory version of this idea.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — Your Position: Provision versus Permission

The disagreement survives only because two different proposals share one name. "Training before parenthood" can mean that preparation should be available, expected and normal; or it can mean that it should be a precondition of permission. Almost every benefit claimed for the idea belongs to the first, and almost every objection raised against it belongs to the second. Once that is seen, the reasonable position is not a compromise between the two views but a clean division of them: accept the first entirely and reject the second entirely. The examiner-facing formulation is short — the state should teach, not forbid.

Philosophical concept: the principle of the least restrictive means does the work here: where a voluntary measure achieves the objective, the coercive measure cannot be justified, because its additional cost in liberty buys nothing. Thaler and Sunstein's choice architecture explains the mechanism — a default, an incentive or a well-placed offer changes behaviour at scale without removing anyone's option to refuse. And a Rawlsian test settles which design is better: judge an arrangement by its effect on the worst-off, and note that a gate excludes precisely the parents who most need support, while an offer reaches them.

Example: Finland is the standing demonstration. Its neuvola clinics — the word means "place of advice" — are free, universal and entirely voluntary, and about 99.7 per cent of pregnant women use them. The reason is instructive: the celebrated maternity package, the "baby box" of clothes and equipment given to every expectant mother since 1949, is granted on condition that she has attended a medical examination at a clinic before the end of the fourth month of pregnancy. Mothers may take a cash grant of €170 instead, and roughly 87 per cent choose the box because it is worth considerably more. Finland thus achieves exactly what the licensing proposal wants — near-universal contact between new parents and structured professional guidance — while assessing nobody, failing nobody and forbidding nobody. Its infant mortality rate is among the lowest in the world. The state made attendance worth choosing rather than illegal to skip, and got a participation rate no examination system could approach.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between protecting children and constraining adults, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that the case for preparing people for parenthood is overwhelming while the case for licensing them is not, since the benefits of the idea are available through provision and the dangers arrive only with permission. The useful question for a government is therefore not whether parents should be better prepared, which no one disputes, but whether the state should be in the business of teaching them or of deciding which of them may proceed.

6. Handling the Counter-Argument (an optional Band 8 move)

The strongest objection to the position above is that voluntary provision reaches everyone except the people who matter most. A figure like 99.7 per cent conceals the remaining fraction, and serious abuse concentrates in precisely the households that avoid contact with services. If that is right, an offer may be adequate for the average family and useless for the extreme one — which is where the entire moral urgency of the question lies. The reply is to concede the case and then confine it. The gap is real, but it is an argument for targeted outreach to identified high-risk families and for the child-protection law that already exists — a mechanism that acts on evidence of actual harm, after the fact, with courts and appeals attached. The proposal under discussion is different: a universal pre-emptive gate imposed on every prospective parent in the country in order to catch a very small number, which is an extremely poor ratio of intrusion to benefit. Raising an objection and answering it in two or three sentences is one of the clearest signals of a Band 8 Task Response, but only attempt it if you can do it briefly.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • The harm principle, reversed — a child is a third party who never consented, so procreation is not purely self-regarding conduct and liberty does not automatically shelter it (Mill).
  • Parenthood as trust, not property — parental authority derives from parental duty and exists for the child's sake, so parents' rights are derivative of children's rights (Locke; O'Neill).
  • The adoption asymmetry — societies already assess parental competence rigorously whenever a child arrives by any route other than birth, so the exemption, not the assessment, is what needs defending.
  • Practical wisdom cannot be examined in advance — phronesis is acquired by habituation in the situation, so a test before parenthood measures articulacy rather than capability (Aristotle).
  • Goodhart's law — once a measure becomes a target it stops measuring what it measured, so applicants would prepare for the test rather than for the child.
  • The "good enough" parent — the standard that matters is adequacy, not excellence, and any examined threshold tends to ratchet upwards over time (Winnicott).
  • No curriculum is neutral — discipline, sleeping arrangements, independence and academic pressure are contested across cultures and classes, so a licence certifies one culture's parenting and pathologises the rest.
  • The enforcement problem — a gate must do something with those who fail it, and a licence with no penalty is not a licence while a licence with a penalty is a horror.
  • The least restrictive means — where a voluntary measure achieves the objective, the coercive one cannot be justified, because the extra liberty it costs buys nothing.
  • Choice architecture — a default, an incentive or a well-timed offer changes behaviour at scale while leaving the option to refuse intact (Thaler and Sunstein).
  • Provision versus permission — two proposals share one name; the benefits belong to the first and the dangers belong to the second, so the sensible position divides them rather than compromises between them.
  • Judge by the worst-off — a gate excludes precisely the parents who most need support, while an offer reaches them (Rawls).

Reminder: do not name philosophers in the exam. The marks are for the reasoning, not the citation — "a test can only examine what a person already is, and patience under exhaustion is not yet present in someone who has never been exhausted by a child" scores, while "as Aristotle argued" does not, and unattributed ideas read as your own thinking.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format, in roughly 330 words. Notice in particular that the writer's position is visible in every paragraph — "I will argue", "The case for compulsion", "I accept this much", "Two objections nevertheless defeat", "In my view", "I therefore conclude" — so the examiner never has to guess whose view is being presented. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box, using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Some people believe that individuals should receive professional training before becoming parents. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

In most countries a person must pass a test to drive a car or foster a child for a weekend, yet may raise a child of their own with no preparation at all. I largely agree that preparation matters, but I will argue that it should be provided rather than required, since the proposal's benefits survive without a gate while its dangers depend on one.

The case for compulsion rests on an inconsistency, not on how demanding parenthood is. Societies already assess parental capacity rigorously whenever a child arrives by any route other than birth: prospective adopters in England undergo roughly six months of interviews, medical checks and formal approval. That a woman gave birth to a particular child is a fact about how he arrived, not evidence about how she will raise him. I accept this much of the argument — the exemption, not the assessment, is what has never been justified.

Two objections nevertheless defeat the licensing version. The first is that a course transmits information but not character. Patience at four in the morning cannot be examined in someone who has never been exhausted by a child, so a test would certify the articulate rather than the capable. The second is decisive: a gate must do something with those who fail it. It can prevent conception, remove the newborn, or nothing at all — and since the first two are the catastrophes eugenic programmes produced, only the third remains, in which case the licence is merely a leaflet.

In my view the dispute survives only because two proposals share one name. Finland assesses nobody, yet almost every expectant mother attends a free advisory clinic, because the maternity grant is conditional on attending one. The state made preparation worth choosing instead of illegal to skip, and reached a participation rate no examination could match.

I therefore conclude that people should indeed be prepared for parenthood, but that the state's proper role is to teach rather than to forbid.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • provided rather than required — offered to everyone but forced on no one; the four-word core of the whole position, and the phrase the essay is built around.
  • compulsion — the use of force or legal obligation to make people do something; the precise noun for the version of the proposal you are rejecting.
  • to rest on an inconsistency — to depend on the fact that two comparable cases are being treated differently; a strong way to open an argument that is not merely emotional.
  • to assess parental capacity — to formally evaluate whether someone is able to raise a child; the neutral technical phrase, far better than "check if they are good parents".
  • by any route other than birth — through adoption, fostering or guardianship rather than by giving birth; an elegant way to name the comparison class.
  • prospective adopters — people applying to adopt a child; standard institutional vocabulary that raises the register immediately.
  • formal approval — official authorisation granted after an assessment process.
  • the exemption, not the assessment, is what has never been justified — a reversal move: instead of defending the new rule, you ask why the existing exception exists. Very high-value rhetorical structure.
  • to transmit information but not character — to be able to teach facts while being unable to instil personal qualities; the compact statement of the epistemic objection.
  • to be examined in advance — to be tested before the relevant situation has arisen; the phrase that makes the timing problem visible.
  • to certify the articulate rather than the capable — to end up approving people who are good at explaining themselves instead of people who are good at the task; a memorable, quotable formulation.
  • decisive — settling the matter conclusively; use it to flag which of your objections carries the most weight.
  • a gate must do something with those who fail it — the named principle of the essay's central objection; a licensing scheme is only real if failing it has consequences.
  • eugenic programmes — historical state policies aimed at controlling who was permitted to reproduce; use factually and briefly, never with outrage.
  • the licence is merely a leaflet — a permission system with no enforcement is only an information campaign; the image that closes the dilemma.
  • two proposals share one name — an ambiguity in the prompt is doing the work of a real disagreement; the standard opening for a resolution paragraph.
  • conditional on — only granted if a stated requirement is met; the exact phrase for describing an incentive rather than a rule.
  • worth choosing instead of illegal to skip — designed so that people want to comply rather than being punished for not complying; the essay's named alternative to licensing.
  • a participation rate — the proportion of eligible people who actually take part; the measurable outcome that settles the argument.
  • to teach rather than to forbid — the final formulation of the position, in six words.
  • a precondition of permission — something that must be satisfied before someone is allowed to proceed; the phrase that names exactly what is being rejected.

6 Self-Review Checklist

  • My introduction paraphrases the prompt AND states my own position explicitly (not saved for the conclusion).
  • I defined in the introduction whether I am discussing training that is offered or training that is required — and I did not slide between the two versions later.
  • My partial agreement is a defined position ("I accept the aim and reject the mechanism"), not a vague one ("I somewhat agree").
  • I gave the case FOR training a full, sympathetic paragraph with a named principle (the adoption asymmetry) and one specific example.
  • I gave the case AGAINST a full paragraph with a named principle (the enforcement problem, or that competence cannot be tested in advance) and one specific example.
  • My position paragraph offers a reasoned resolution (provision versus permission) with an example.
  • I kept the essay about the POLICY — I said who would design the course, who would judge it, or what would happen to those who failed, rather than only describing how hard parenting is.
  • I used no personal anecdote — no "my own mother never had training and raised me well", which is a sample of one.
  • I stated any historical material (sterilisation programmes, forced child removals) factually and briefly, without an outraged tone.
  • My conclusion is short, uses a linking word, and introduces no new examples.
  • I used different real-world examples than the model essay above and did not name any philosophers.
  • I wrote at least 270 words across four to five paragraphs.
