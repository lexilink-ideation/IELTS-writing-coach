C2 Task 2

Unit 21: Charities — Help Anywhere, or Focus at Home? — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Discussion Essay (Discuss Both Views + Opinion, Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is a Discussion (Discuss Both Views + Opinion) essay at its most sophisticated. The task type matters: "Discuss both views" is not the same as "agree or disagree." Both views must be developed fairly, each in its own paragraph and presented at its strongest, before your own opinion is defended. An essay that argues one side and waves the other away is capped at Band 6 for Task Response, however good the English. A Band 9 response weighs two competing accounts of what creates a duty to help — the severity of the need itself versus the relationship between giver and receiver — before landing on a clear, qualified position. Every paragraph still follows the core discipline you have practised: an introduction with a background statement and a visible thesis, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + signal both views + state your position. Paragraph 2 (View A): need is need — distance is morally arbitrary and money goes further where poverty is deepest, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (View B): obligation follows relationship — special duties to fellow citizens; local charities know and answer to those they serve, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Your position): a division of moral labour — domestic hardship is chiefly the government's job; charities belong where no capable state exists, one specific example. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): restate the tension + confident, qualified final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • Develop both views fairly. Give the view you ultimately reject a full, sympathetic paragraph — present it at its strongest, never as a straw man.
  • State your position in the introduction as well as the conclusion. Examiners look for a clear, consistent stance throughout, not a verdict saved for the last sentence.
  • Name a principle in plain English (impartial need, special obligation, division of labour) — this makes the reasoning visible and supports both coherence and task response.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, figures) beats breadth.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 270-310 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question, signal both views, and state your position.
  • Body Paragraph 1 (View A) — need is need: distance is morally arbitrary, and money goes further where poverty is deepest.
  • Body Paragraph 2 (View B) — obligation follows relationship: special duties to fellow citizens; local charities know and answer to those they serve.
  • Body Paragraph 3 (Your position) — a division of labour: domestic hardship is chiefly the government's job; charities belong where no capable state exists.
  • Conclusion — restate the tension + a confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that you will weigh two competing accounts of what generates a duty to help — the severity of the need itself, versus the relationship between giver and receiver — before landing on a clear position. Your opinion should be visible here, not saved as a surprise for the conclusion.

Example paraphrase: "Charitable organisations must constantly decide where their limited resources should go. While some maintain that aid should follow need wherever it is most acute, others argue that donors owe a special obligation to their own communities. This essay will examine both positions before arguing that charities do the most good where no functioning state is able to act, which in practice usually means overseas."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — View A: Need Is Need, Wherever It Occurs

The first view rests on a simple moral intuition: suffering does not become less real because it happens far away. Where a child is born is an accident of birth, and it is hard to explain why that accident should determine whether she receives help. A practical consideration reinforces the moral one — the same donation stretches much further where poverty is deepest, so giving that follows need rather than nationality relieves considerably more suffering per pound spent.

Philosophical concept: Moral impartialism (the argument that physical distance is a morally arbitrary feature, no more relevant than eye colour; Mozi's ancient doctrine of 兼爱 — impartial care extended equally to all) combined with the economic principle of diminishing marginal utility: a fixed sum produces far more welfare among the very poor than among the moderately poor. A stronger version holds that global poverty is partly sustained by the trade rules and resource purchases of wealthy nations, which makes cross-border giving a matter of repairing an injustice rather than of optional generosity.

Example: The WHO's smallpox eradication campaign was financed substantially by donors in countries where the disease had already disappeared — international contributions of around US$98 million, alongside roughly US$200 million from the affected countries themselves — and by 1980 had eliminated a disease that was still killing between 1.5 and 2 million people a year when the programme began. No domestic donation could match that return, and it was achieved precisely because the giving followed the need rather than the borders.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — View B: Obligation Follows Relationship

The opposing view holds that moral duty is not only a function of need but also of relationship. Just as parents owe more to their own children than to strangers', fellow citizens stand in a web of shared institutions, taxes and mutual dependence that generates genuine obligations to one another. Supporters of this position also point out that hardship at home is easily overlooked, and that local charities understand their communities and can be held to account by them in a way that distant operations cannot.

Philosophical concept: Associative duties — the argument that obligations arise from membership and reciprocity rather than from abstract humanity alone; Mencius's 爱有差等 ("love with gradations"), which extends care outward from those nearest us. A practical strand reinforces this: the accountability critique of large-scale foreign aid warns that distant "planners" ultimately answer to their donors rather than to their recipients, whereas local actors possess the knowledge and the accountability that make help actually work.

Example: In the United Kingdom, food banks in the Trussell network distributed almost 2.9 million emergency food parcels between April 2024 and March 2025, more than a million of them for children — evidence that severe deprivation persists inside one of the world's wealthiest countries, and that locally rooted charities, run by neighbours who know the families they serve, are often best placed to find and reach it.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — Your Position: A Division of Moral Labour

These positions can be reconciled by asking not where the need is, but who else is capable of meeting it. Developed countries already possess tax systems, welfare programmes and elected governments whose proper business is domestic hardship; where a food bank is quietly doing the state's work, the real remedy is political rather than charitable. Across much of the world, by contrast, no such institution exists, and a charity is not one option among several but the only actor available. On this reasoning, charities should concentrate where institutional capacity is absent — usually abroad — without implying that need at home does not matter.

Philosophical concept: A division of moral labour, closely related to the principle of subsidiarity: responsibility should sit with the institution best equipped to discharge it, and charity should fill the gaps that institutions cannot. The notion of an imperfect duty supports this conclusion — we are strictly obliged to help others, but retain latitude in choosing whom, so neither choice is a moral failure. Hierocles' image of concentric circles adds the practical wisdom that moral concern begins with local ties, but that a mature ethics deliberately draws the outer circles inward.

Example: BRAC, founded in Bangladesh in 1972 and now among the largest non-governmental organisations in the world, was built with substantial funding from donors in wealthy countries yet designed and delivered entirely by Bangladeshi staff — and today finances the greater part of its own work through the social enterprises it created. It shows that the real choice is not between giving at home and giving abroad, but between money and delivery, which can come from different places, and that well-directed foreign giving can build local capacity that eventually no longer needs it.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between impartial need and special obligation, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that although the pull towards one's own community is understandable and domestic hardship is real, charities achieve most where no government is able to act, and citizens should be pressing their own state, rather than their charities, to address poverty at home.

6. Handling the Counter-Argument (an optional Band 8 move)

The famous "drowning child" comparison — you would ruin an expensive suit to save a child drowning in front of you, so why not send the same sum abroad? — is usually cited to defend the impartial view, but a sharp student can turn it around: the case seems to show that immediacy is exactly what creates urgency. The strongest reply is that the pond is a rescue (one identifiable victim, one moment, a certain outcome), whereas poverty at home and abroad alike is chronic aid (statistical victims, uncertain outcomes). Distance changes what you are able to know and verify; it does not change what is morally at stake. Raising an objection and answering it in two or three sentences is one of the clearest signals of a Band 8 Task Response — but only attempt it if you can do it briefly.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Moral impartialism — distance is morally arbitrary; suffering counts equally wherever it occurs (Singer; Mozi's 兼爱).
  • Diminishing marginal utility — the same sum of money relieves far more suffering where poverty is deepest.
  • Rectification rather than generosity — global poverty is partly produced by rich-country institutions, so aid repays a debt (Pogge).
  • Associative duties — obligation arises from membership, reciprocity and shared institutions (Miller, Walzer; Mencius's 爱有差等).
  • The accountability critique — distant aid answers to donors, local action answers to recipients (Easterly).
  • Division of moral labour / subsidiarity — charity belongs where no capable institution exists; domestic hardship is chiefly the state's duty.
  • Imperfect duty — helping is obligatory, but the choice of whom to help retains latitude (Kant).

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format, in roughly 300 words. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Some people think charity organisations should help people in great need, wherever they live. However, others think that they should concentrate on helping people in their own countries. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

Charitable organisations must constantly decide where their limited resources should go. Some maintain that aid should follow need wherever it is most acute; others insist that donors owe a special obligation to their own communities. This essay examines both before arguing that charities do most good where no functioning state can act, usually overseas.

The first view rests on a simple intuition: suffering does not become less real because it happens far away. Where a child is born is a matter of chance that should not decide whether she is helped, and the same sum stretches much further where poverty is deepest. The WHO's smallpox campaign, funded largely by countries where the disease had already vanished, eradicated by 1980 an illness once killing up to two million people a year — a return no domestic gift could match.

The opposing view holds that duty follows relationship as well as need. Just as parents owe more to their own children than to strangers, fellow citizens share institutions and mutual dependence that create real obligations, and local charities are accountable in a way distant operations are not. In Britain, Trussell food banks handed out almost 2.9 million emergency parcels in one year, proof that deprivation persists even in a wealthy country.

The two can be reconciled by asking not where the need lies but who else can meet it. Developed countries have welfare systems and elected governments whose proper business is domestic hardship; elsewhere, no such institution exists, and a charity is the only actor available. BRAC, built in Bangladesh with foreign funding yet run by Bangladeshis, shows that well-directed giving can create local capacity that eventually needs no outside help.

In conclusion, although the pull towards one's own community is understandable, charities achieve most where no government can act, and citizens should press their own state, not their charities, to tackle poverty at home.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • to follow need (rather than borders) — to direct aid according to where suffering is greatest, not according to nationality or location.
  • where need is most acute — where hardship is most severe and urgent; a formal way to identify the deepest poverty.
  • a special obligation — a duty owed to a particular group (such as one's own community) that goes beyond the general duty owed to everyone.
  • morally arbitrary — irrelevant to what is right or wrong; the essay argues distance is arbitrary in this sense, like eye colour.
  • an accident of birth — a circumstance (such as where one is born) that is a matter of chance rather than desert.
  • a donation stretches further — a fixed sum of money achieves more where costs are low and poverty is deep; the idea of diminishing marginal utility in plain English.
  • duty follows relationship — the view that obligation arises from ties of membership and reciprocity, not from need alone (associative duties).
  • mutual dependence — a situation in which people rely on one another, here through shared institutions and taxes.
  • to be held to account — to be made answerable for one's actions; the essay notes local charities are accountable to those they serve.
  • severe deprivation — a serious lack of the basic things people need to live, such as food or shelter.
  • a division of (moral) labour — an arrangement in which different actors take responsibility for different tasks; here, the state for home, charities for abroad.
  • whose proper business is (something) — the matter that is rightly a particular body's responsibility; here, domestic hardship is the government's.
  • institutional capacity — the ability of established bodies (governments, welfare systems) to carry out a task; charities matter most where it is absent.
  • the only actor available — the sole organisation able to act in a given situation, so its role is not optional.
  • to build local capacity — to develop a community's own ability to help itself, so outside aid is eventually no longer needed.
  • the pull towards one's own community — the natural tendency to feel a stronger duty to those close to us; acknowledged but not treated as decisive.
