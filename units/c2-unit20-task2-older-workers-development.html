C2 Task 2

Unit 20: Employers Hiring Older Workers — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Positive/Negative Development Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is a Positive or Negative Development essay at its most sophisticated. Rather than flatly labelling the trend "positive" or "negative," a Band 9 response interrogates a word the prompt quietly hides — willing — and weighs the recognition of experience against the risk of exploitation before landing on a qualified position: the development is positive, but only where continued work is genuinely chosen rather than forced. Every paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practised throughout the curriculum: an introduction with a background statement and a clear thesis, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence and restate your position, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + thesis statement (a qualified, largely-positive position). Paragraph 2 (The case for): experience a firm cannot buy + the old miscounted as dependants, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (The case against): whose "willingness" is this? — older workers hired because they are cheap and cannot refuse, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (The resolving distinction): chosen work versus forced work + your reasoned position, one specific example. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summary + confident, qualified final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • A qualified verdict ("positive, but only where the work remains genuinely voluntary") scores better than a flat positive/negative — it shows the nuance examiners look for at Band 7+.
  • Interrogate a loaded word in the prompt. Here it is willing: asking why an employer is willing turns an ordinary essay into an analytical one.
  • Name a principle (tacit knowledge, the dependency ratio, commodification, the voluntariness test) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, timeframes) beats breadth.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + state a qualified thesis (largely positive, but conditional).
  • Body Paragraph 1 — the case FOR: experience is knowledge a firm cannot buy, and an ageing world miscounts the old as dependants.
  • Body Paragraph 2 — the case AGAINST: whose "willingness" is this? Older workers may be hired because they are cheap and cannot refuse.
  • Body Paragraph 3 — the resolving distinction: chosen work versus forced work + your reasoned position.
  • Conclusion — summarize + confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that the answer depends on something the question hides: whether these older workers are being valued or merely being used. Announce that you will weigh the recognition of experience against the risk of exploitation before landing on a qualified position. Avoid a flat "this is positive" thesis; IELTS examiners reward a nuanced stance in positive/negative essays as much as a decisive one.

Example paraphrase: "An increasing number of employers are now prepared to recruit staff well beyond the conventional retirement age. This essay will examine why this trend reflects a welcome recognition of experience, why it may equally signal that older people simply cannot afford to stop working, and will argue that the development is positive only where continued employment remains genuinely voluntary."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For: Experience, and the Miscounted Dependant

There is a strong case that this trend is overdue. Much of what an experienced worker knows — when a rule should be bent, how to read a difficult client, why the last three attempts at a project failed — cannot be written into a manual, and it leaves a company permanently when that person does. There is also a demographic case, though it must be put carefully. Economists divide a society into workers and dependants at a fixed age, but that line was drawn when people died far younger, and it sorts people by their birthday rather than by what they can actually do: a healthy sixty-six-year-old is counted as a dependant even while working. Employers who hire past retirement age are correcting that mistake rather than carrying a burden.

Philosophical concept: Two ideas combine. Practical wisdom and tacit knowledge (Aristotle's phronesis — the judgment that comes only from long practice; Michael Polanyi's observation that "we know more than we can tell") explains why experience is an asset that cannot be downloaded or trained overnight. The dependency ratio as a definitional artefact adds the demographic dimension: the "burden" of an ageing society is partly manufactured by where we draw the line between producer and dependant. Move the line and the crisis changes size, without a single person changing. Note the direction of this argument: the old have been wrongly classified as unable to contribute, NOT that they must be made to.

Example: In 2007 BMW rebuilt one production line at its Dingolfing plant around an older workforce, making around seventy small ergonomic changes — wooden flooring, adjustable benches, magnifying lenses — for roughly €40,000. Within a year the line's productivity had risen by about 7 per cent, matching lines staffed by younger workers, while absenteeism fell from 7 to 2 per cent, showing that the supposed frailty of older employees is largely a design problem rather than a fact about them.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case Against: Whose "Willingness" Is This?

Yet the word "willing" deserves suspicion. Employers may prefer older applicants not because they respect their judgment but because they are cheap, easily rehired on fixed-term or part-time contracts that fall outside the protections their career jobs carried, and badly placed to walk away, since a worker of sixty-three who loses a job is far less likely to find another than one of thirty. On this reading the trend is not a triumph of merit over prejudice but the extension of paid labour into the last unmonetised stretch of a human life. A society in which people are still working at seventy-eight is not obviously a kinder one.

Philosophical concept: Two strands. The commodification critique (Marx on labour power as a commodity, and the broader argument that market logic tends to colonise areas of life once held outside it) holds that what looks like inclusion may be the conversion of old age itself into working time. The leisure tradition (Aristotle's scholē — the free time in which a human life is actually lived; Keynes, who predicted in 1930 that rising productivity would bring his grandchildren a fifteen-hour week) holds that the point of economic progress is freedom from labour, so rising elderly employment may measure the failure of pensions rather than the enlightenment of employers.

Example: South Korea has the highest old-age poverty rate in the OECD, with 39.8 per cent of those aged 65 and over living in relative poverty in 2023; a government survey in 2024 identified roughly one in every 650 Koreans over 65 collecting waste cardboard for a living, at an average age of 78 and an average monthly income of about 766,000 won (some US$550) — evidence that a high rate of elderly employment can reflect destitution rather than opportunity.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — The Resolving Distinction: Chosen Work or Forced Work

The two positions can be reconciled by noticing that they describe the same statistic under different conditions. A person of sixty-eight in paid work may be exercising a valued capacity or enduring a necessity, and the outward fact is identical in both cases; what distinguishes them is whether that person could stop. The trend is therefore positive where a secure pension makes continued work a genuine choice, and negative where the pension has failed and work is the only alternative to poverty. The practical test for any society is simple: could its older workers afford to leave tomorrow?

Philosophical concept: This rests on the voluntariness test — the same fact (working at sixty-eight) is a capability exercised or a necessity endured depending on whether the person could stop. It draws on the capability approach (Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum): wellbeing is not just income but the real freedom to do and to be, so the value of continued work lies precisely in its being optional rather than compelled.

Example: The Netherlands, whose pension system was ranked the strongest in the world in the 2025 Mercer CFA Institute Global Pension Index, has also seen employment among people aged 55 to 64 rise from under 40 per cent in 2000 to over 70 per cent today — showing that a generous retirement floor does not stop people working longer, but does mean that those who do so are choosing to, which is precisely what makes the Dutch case positive and the Korean one troubling.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between experience recognised and desperation exploited, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that the willingness of employers to hire older workers is a positive development in itself, but only remains so in societies where those workers retain the freedom to stop.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Practical wisdom and tacit knowledge — experience produces judgment that cannot be codified or taught quickly (Aristotle's phronesis; Polanyi's "we know more than we can tell").
  • The dependency ratio as a definitional artefact — an ageing society's "burden" depends partly on where the line between producer and dependant is drawn, and that line was set when people died far younger. Argue that the old are miscounted, never that they must be made to work.
  • The fairness argument everyone has a stake in — age is the one characteristic every person eventually acquires, so a hiring norm that excludes the old is one each of us is building for our own future self (a Rawlsian "veil of ignorance" argument): unjust, not merely irrational.
  • The commodification critique — apparent inclusion may be the conversion of the last free stretch of life into working time (Marx and the market-limits tradition).
  • The leisure tradition — the purpose of economic progress is freedom from labour, so more elderly work may signal pension failure (Aristotle's scholē; Keynes's fifteen-hour week).
  • The voluntariness test — the same fact (working at sixty-eight) is a capability exercised or a necessity endured depending on whether the person could stop.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format, in roughly 300 words. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Some employers are willing to hire older workers. Is this a positive or negative development?

An increasing number of employers are now prepared to recruit staff well beyond the conventional retirement age. In my view this is a positive development, because it recognises experience a company cannot easily replace and corrects the way ageing societies miscount their older members — but it remains positive only where such work is genuinely chosen rather than forced by poverty.

The strongest argument in its favour is that experience is an asset no manual can capture. An employee who knows when a rule can be bent carries judgement that leaves permanently the day they retire. There is also a demographic point: economists split society into workers and dependants at a fixed age that was drawn when people died far younger, so a healthy sixty-six-year-old is filed as a dependant even while working. When BMW rebuilt a production line around older staff at its Dingolfing plant, output rose and absenteeism fell, suggesting their supposed frailty was a design problem, not a fact.

Yet the word "willing" deserves suspicion. Employers may favour older applicants not out of respect but because they are cheap, easily rehired on insecure contracts, and poorly placed to refuse. On this reading the trend simply extends paid labour into the last free stretch of a human life. South Korea, where nearly forty per cent of over-sixty-fives live in poverty and some collect waste cardboard to survive, shows that high elderly employment can reflect desperation rather than opportunity.

These positions describe the same fact under different conditions; what separates them is whether the worker could stop. Where a secure pension makes work a real choice, as in the Netherlands, the trend is welcome; where the pension has failed, it is not.

In conclusion, employers' willingness to hire older workers is positive in itself, but only in societies where those workers retain the genuine freedom to stop.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • beyond the conventional retirement age — older than the age at which people usually stop working; a formal way to describe hiring the elderly.
  • a qualified position ("positive only where...") — an opinion that holds true under a stated condition rather than absolutely; the mark of a nuanced verdict.
  • to miscount (someone as) — to classify someone wrongly; here, to treat capable older people as economically inactive.
  • a dependant — a person who relies on others for financial support rather than earning; the essay argues many older people are wrongly placed in this category.
  • tacit knowledge / judgement a manual cannot capture — practical know-how gained from experience that cannot be fully written down or taught quickly.
  • the dependency ratio — the proportion of non-working people (young and old) to working-age people; the essay notes where its dividing line is drawn is a convention.
  • the word "willing" deserves suspicion — a phrase that interrogates a loaded term in the prompt, asking why employers are willing rather than taking it at face value.
  • insecure contracts — fixed-term or part-time arrangements that lack the protections and stability of a permanent job.
  • poorly placed to refuse — in a weak bargaining position, so unable to turn down bad terms.
  • to commodify / the last free stretch of a human life — to turn something once outside the market (here, old age) into paid working time.
  • to reflect desperation rather than opportunity — to be caused by hardship rather than genuine choice.
  • the voluntariness test ("whether the worker could stop") — the criterion that distinguishes chosen work from forced work: a person exercising a capacity versus enduring a necessity.
  • a secure pension / a generous retirement floor — a reliable guaranteed income in old age that makes continued work a real choice rather than a necessity.
  • the genuine freedom to stop — the real ability to leave paid work without falling into poverty; the essay's decisive condition.

6 Self-Review Checklist

  • My introduction has a background statement (paraphrase) and a thesis stating a qualified, largely-positive position ("positive, but only where...").
  • My "case for" paragraph names a principle in plain English (tacit knowledge or the dependency ratio) and includes one specific, well-developed example.
  • My "case against" paragraph interrogates the word "willing" and names a different principle (commodification or the leisure tradition) with one specific example.
  • My resolving paragraph applies the voluntariness test (could the worker stop?) and states my own reasoned position.
  • My conclusion is short, uses a linking word, and introduces no new examples.
  • I used different real-world examples than the model essay above.
  • I argued the old are miscounted as dependants — never that solvency requires them to keep working.
