C2 Task 2

Unit 18: Why the Quality of Life Is Declining in Major Cities — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Cause & Solution Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is a Cause & Solution essay at its most sophisticated. The question asks two things — why life in big cities is getting worse, and what can be done — so a Band 9 response gives roughly equal weight to both, and makes sure every solution clearly answers a cause you named. Rather than offering an opinion, you name the direction of travel: a clear road map of causes followed by realistic, matching remedies. Each paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practiced throughout the curriculum: an introduction that paraphrases and signals structure, body paragraphs that open with a clear topic sentence, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): paraphrase + road map (name the causes, then signal solutions). Paragraph 2 (Cause 1 — structural): growth over wellbeing and the overused commons, one named principle, one example. Paragraph 3 (Cause 2 — social): crowding, alienation and lost community, one named principle, one example. Paragraph 4 (Solutions): plan for the many, protect the worst-off, rethink what cities are for. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summarise causes and remedies in one confident sentence, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • Answer BOTH questions explicitly — give causes and solutions roughly equal weight, and make each solution visibly match a cause you named.
  • Name a principle (growth-versus-flourishing, the tragedy of the commons, the greatest good, protect the worst-off) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, figures) beats breadth.
  • Keep solutions concrete and realistic (public transport, affordable housing, green space) rather than vague ("the government should help") — specific measures score higher.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + signal that you will explain the main causes and then propose realistic solutions.
  • Body Paragraph 1 — Causes (structural): cities optimise for growth, not for a good life, and shared resources are overused.
  • Body Paragraph 2 — Causes (social): rapid, crowded urban living erodes community and belonging.
  • Body Paragraph 3 — Solutions: plan for the many and protect the worst-off, plus a cultural shift in what we value.
  • Conclusion — summarise the key causes and the most effective solutions in one confident sentence.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then make clear that you will do the two things the question asks: identify why life in big cities is getting worse, and suggest workable ways to reverse it. In a cause-and-solution essay the examiner rewards a clear road map, so name the direction of travel rather than giving an opinion. Keep the framing precise: the problem is not simply "too many people" but how cities are organised and what they are organised for.

Example paraphrase: "It is increasingly argued that life in many of the world's largest cities is becoming less pleasant and less healthy. This essay will argue that the decline stems mainly from cities being built to maximise economic growth rather than human wellbeing, and from the overcrowding that weakens community life, before suggesting that the most effective remedies are planning that prioritises the majority and the least advantaged, together with a shift in what urban life is understood to be for."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — Causes: Growth Without Wellbeing, and the Overused Commons

The first cause is that modern cities are designed to maximise output rather than to help people live well. Success is measured in GDP, property values and productivity, so investment flows to what generates money — offices, roads, high-rise development — while the things that make daily life good, such as clean air, green space, short commutes and time for family, are treated as afterthoughts. A second, related cause is that the shared resources a city depends on are steadily degraded: when everyone rationally drives, builds higher or consumes more, the common goods of clean air, quiet, road space and public land are eroded for all.

Philosophical concept: Two ideas explain this. Aristotle's notion of eudaimonia — that the point of a good life is human flourishing, not the accumulation of wealth — exposes a confusion of means and ends: cities chase growth as if it were the goal, when it is only a means to a life worth living. Alongside it, the "tragedy of the commons" (Garrett Hardin) shows why individually rational choices — one more car, one more tower — add up to collective harm, because no single person bears the full cost of the shared resource they deplete.

Example: Cities such as Beijing and Delhi have posted rapid economic growth alongside hazardous air pollution and some of the world's longest commutes — a vivid case of prosperity rising while everyday quality of life falls, because growth, not wellbeing, was the organising aim.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — Causes: Crowding, Alienation and the Loss of Community

A further cause is social rather than physical. As cities grow rapidly and densely, people are packed together in space yet pushed apart in their relationships: neighbours are strangers, work absorbs the day, and the informal bonds that once made a place feel like home thin out. High rents and constant mobility make these bonds harder still to form. This matters because much of what people mean by "quality of life" is not material at all — it is safety, familiarity and belonging — and these are precisely what anonymous, transient urban living tends to erode.

Philosophical concept: The idea of alienation (associated with Karl Marx, and echoed by later urban thinkers) captures this paradox: modern city life can leave people surrounded by millions yet feeling isolated and disconnected from others and from a sense of place. It reframes declining quality of life as a loss of community and meaning, not merely of infrastructure — a decline that no amount of new building alone can fix.

Example: Studies of large, fast-growing cities repeatedly find higher rates of loneliness and anxiety than smaller towns despite greater wealth and amenities — evidence that crowding without community leaves people materially better off but socially poorer, which is exactly the decline the question describes.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — Solutions: Plan for the Many, Protect the Worst-Off, Rethink What Cities Are For

If the causes are the wrong priorities and weakened community, the solutions follow directly. First, governments should judge urban policy by how much it improves life for the greatest number — investing in reliable public transport, green space, clean air and affordable housing rather than prestige projects that benefit a few. Second, and as a check on the first, cities should be measured by how they treat their most vulnerable residents, guaranteeing decent housing and services to those at the bottom rather than optimising for the average. Finally, part of the answer is cultural: recognising that beyond a point wellbeing depends less on ever-greater consumption than on time, relationships and a liveable environment.

Philosophical concept: Three principles support this. Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) gives the aim: design the city for "the greatest good for the greatest number," which justifies public goods over private prestige. Rawls's justice as fairness adds the safeguard: judge a society by the position of its worst-off members, so that the poor are not sacrificed to the average. And the Stoic-Epicurean insight into desire — that much dissatisfaction comes from insatiable wants rather than real needs — grounds the cultural shift, suggesting solutions are partly individual, not only governmental.

Example: Cities such as Vienna and Copenhagen — with heavy public investment in affordable housing, transport and green space — consistently top global quality-of-life rankings, showing that planning for the many and protecting the least advantaged, rather than for growth alone, is what actually raises how well people live.

5. Conclusion

Restate the two main causes — cities built to maximise growth rather than wellbeing, and dense, transient living that erodes community — then give a brief, confident summary of the remedy: that quality of life is best restored when governments plan for the majority and the worst-off, invest in the shared goods of clean air, transport, housing and green space, and when society rebalances what it treats as a good life away from endless growth and toward things that genuinely make daily life worth living.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Eudaimonia (Aristotle) — the goal of life is human flourishing, so cities that chase growth as an end in itself mistake the means for the goal.
  • Tragedy of the commons (Hardin) — individually rational choices (more cars, more building) collectively degrade shared goods such as clean air and public space.
  • Alienation (Marx) — dense, rapid urban life can leave people surrounded by many yet isolated, so decline is a loss of community, not just of infrastructure.
  • Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) — design the city for the greatest good for the greatest number, favouring public goods over prestige projects.
  • Justice as fairness (Rawls) — judge a city by how it treats its worst-off, guaranteeing housing and services to the most vulnerable.
  • Stoic / Epicurean view of desire — wellbeing beyond a point depends on limiting insatiable wants, making part of the solution cultural and individual.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below (around 300 words), written in the paraphrase-plus-road-map / topic-sentence / matched-solution format. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: In many major cities, the quality of life is declining. What are the causes of this problem? What solutions can you suggest?

It is increasingly argued that life in the world's largest cities is deteriorating. This essay will argue that the decline stems chiefly from cities being organised around economic growth rather than wellbeing, and from overcrowding that corrodes community, before proposing remedies centred on planning for the majority and the worst-off alongside a cultural shift in what cities are for.

The first cause is structural: cities are engineered to maximise output rather than to help people live well. Because success is measured in GDP and property values, investment flows toward offices and towers, while clean air, green space and short commutes become afterthoughts. This is compounded by a "tragedy of the commons": when everyone rationally drives or builds higher, shared goods such as clean air are degraded for all. Beijing and Delhi, for instance, have posted rapid growth alongside hazardous pollution and punishing commutes.

A second cause is social. As cities swell, people are packed together yet pushed apart: neighbours are strangers and high rents thin the bonds that make a place feel like home. This matters because much of what we mean by quality of life — safety, familiarity, belonging — is not material at all. Studies repeatedly record higher loneliness in large cities than in smaller towns, a symptom of deep alienation.

The remedies follow directly. Governments should judge urban policy by how far it improves life for the greatest number, investing in transport, affordable housing and green space over prestige projects, while a Rawlsian safeguard demands they also protect the worst-off. Part of the answer is cultural: recognising that beyond a point wellbeing depends less on consumption than on time and relationships. Vienna and Copenhagen, with heavy investment in housing and transport, consistently top quality-of-life rankings.

In conclusion, urban life declines mainly because cities pursue growth over wellbeing and dense living erodes community, and is best restored when governments plan for the many and the vulnerable and society rethinks what makes a good life.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • deteriorating — becoming progressively worse in quality or condition.
  • stems from — is caused by or originates in; a precise way to name a cause.
  • organised around — structured so that one aim (here, growth) is the central priority everything else serves.
  • maximise output — produce the greatest possible economic result; here, treated as a city's main goal.
  • afterthoughts — things considered only later and given low priority, rather than planned for from the start.
  • the tragedy of the commons — the idea that when a shared resource is free to use, each person's rational self-interest leads collectively to its ruin.
  • shared goods — resources that belong to everyone in common, such as clean air, quiet and public space.
  • degraded — reduced in quality or value; worn down.
  • punishing commutes — daily journeys to work that are exhausting because they are so long or difficult.
  • packed together yet pushed apart — physically crowded but socially distant; the paradox of dense city life.
  • alienation — a feeling of isolation and disconnection from other people and from a sense of place.
  • the greatest number — the largest possible group of people; the utilitarian test of whether a policy is good.
  • prestige projects — expensive, high-profile developments valued mainly for status rather than for broad public benefit.
  • a Rawlsian safeguard — a protection, drawn from the philosopher Rawls, that a society should be judged by how well it treats its worst-off members.
  • the worst-off — the most disadvantaged and vulnerable people in a society.
  • rebalances / rethinks what makes a good life — changes what a society values, shifting away from endless consumption toward time, relationships and a liveable environment.
