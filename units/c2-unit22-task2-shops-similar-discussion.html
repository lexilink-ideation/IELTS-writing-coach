C2 Task 2

Unit 22: Are Shops and Products Becoming Too Similar? — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Discussion Essay (Discuss Both Views + Opinion, Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is a Discussion (Discuss Both Views + Opinion) essay at its most sophisticated. The task type matters: "Discuss both views" is not the same as "agree or disagree." Both views must be developed fairly, each in its own paragraph and presented at its strongest, before your own opinion is defended. An essay that argues one side and waves the other away is capped at Band 6 for Task Response, however good the English. A Band 9 response weighs two competing accounts of what shopping is actually for — the delivery of reliable goods, versus the sustaining of distinctive places and cultures — before landing on a clear, qualified position. Every paragraph still follows the core discipline you have practised: an introduction with a background statement and a visible thesis, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + signal both views + state your position. Paragraph 2 (View A): sameness is reliability — standardisation removes risk from ordinary purchases and spreads dependable quality beyond the well-off, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (View B): sameness empties places of meaning — interchangeable high streets erode identity, community and genuine choice, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Your position): function versus meaning — uniformity is a virtue in goods valued for what they do, a loss in goods and places valued for what they mean, one specific example. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): restate the tension + confident, qualified final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • Develop both views fairly. Give the view you ultimately reject a full, sympathetic paragraph — present it at its strongest, never as a straw man.
  • State your position in the introduction as well as the conclusion. Examiners look for a clear, consistent stance throughout, not a verdict saved for the last sentence.
  • Name a principle in plain English (reliability, distinctiveness, function versus meaning) — this makes the reasoning visible and supports both coherence and task response.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, figures) beats breadth.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 270-310 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question, signal both views, and state your position.
  • Body Paragraph 1 (View A) — sameness is reliability: standardisation removes risk from ordinary purchases, spreads dependable quality beyond the well-off, and makes unfamiliar places easier to navigate.
  • Body Paragraph 2 (View B) — sameness empties places of meaning: interchangeable high streets erode identity, community and genuine choice.
  • Body Paragraph 3 (Your position) — distinguish function from meaning: uniformity is a virtue in goods valued for what they do, and a loss in goods and places valued for what they mean.
  • Conclusion — restate the tension + a confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that you will weigh two competing accounts of what shopping is for — the delivery of reliable goods, versus the sustaining of distinctive places and cultures — before landing on a clear position. Your opinion should be visible here, not saved as a surprise for the conclusion.

Example paraphrase: "Whether in Shanghai or Santiago, shoppers increasingly encounter the same chains selling the same goods behind the same shopfronts. Some regard this convergence as a welcome guarantee of quality and affordability, while others see in it the slow erasure of local character. This essay will examine both positions before arguing that uniformity is beneficial where a product's value lies in what it does, and damaging where its value lies in what it means."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — View A: Sameness Is Reliability

The first view rests on the observation that most of what we buy is valued for its dependability rather than its character. A standardised product carries a guarantee: the buyer knows in advance what it will do, and knows whom to hold responsible if it does not. Scale certainly lowers prices, but the deeper benefit is that risk is removed from ordinary purchases. The gain is also quietly egalitarian, since a shopper in a small provincial town can now buy goods as reliable as those sold in the capital. A second strand of the same view looks beyond the shop counter: because the same goods now appear everywhere, people arriving in a foreign country already know how to function in it.

Philosophical concept: The utilitarian case for predictability, reinforced by the economics of trust: a recognisable brand functions as a portable promise, a reputation the seller would lose by cheating, which allows strangers to transact without verifying anything themselves. This is the constructive half of what Max Weber called rationalisation — the replacement of personal acquaintance by impersonal, reliable systems. A Rawlsian test sharpens the point: judging an arrangement by its effect on the worst-off favours uniformity, because distinctive local retail often meant that the quality of what you could buy depended on where you happened to live and what you could afford.

Example: The World Health Organization estimates that around one in ten medical products circulating in low- and middle-income countries is substandard or falsified, and that between 72,000 and 169,000 children may die each year of pneumonia treated with such antibiotics. These are markets where products are anything but uniform and few sellers answer for what they sell. The comparison shows what standardised, brand-accountable supply chains actually deliver: not blandness, but the conversion of a purchase from a gamble into a guarantee.

Developing the point — shared surfaces and mutual understanding. The reliability argument can be extended from the economic into the cultural, and an essay that does so well stands out. Because the same shops and products now appear in most countries, a traveller, student or migrant arrives already knowing how to buy medicine, pay for a bus or read a label, so the disorientation of entering an unfamiliar society is considerably reduced. The same convergence supplies a shared stock of references — the same films, brands and devices — which gives people from opposite ends of the world an immediate way to begin talking to one another. Nor is such contact trivial: research on intergroup contact consistently finds that exposure to another group reduces prejudice, and that the ideal conditions once thought necessary for this turn out merely to help.

The lineage of the idea is respectable. Doux commerce held that trade softens manners and inclines nations towards peace, and Kant argued in Perpetual Peace that commercial republics come to find war unprofitable. Its contemporary form is the claim that consumer and economic integration has made serious conflict too costly for governments to contemplate.

The limits of the argument matter more than the argument itself, and a student who states them earns credit for judgement rather than losing it for concession. Consuming the same goods in parallel is not the same as meeting anyone: convergence creates the conditions in which contact might occur rather than the contact itself. Familiarity also fails to prevent conflict — some of the last century's worst violence took place between neighbours who shared a language, a market and a television schedule. There is even a risk running the other way, since shared surfaces can produce a false sense of familiarity: a person who buys what we buy is easily assumed to reason as we do about family, authority and obligation. Acknowledged difference makes people careful, whereas assumed similarity makes them careless.

The practical rule for the exam: make the modest claim and leave the ambitious one alone. Arguing that a shared commercial vocabulary makes foreign places navigable and gives strangers somewhere to begin is defensible and adds a dimension to the paragraph; arguing that it prevents wars invites the examiner to disagree, and disagreement is the enemy of Task Response. The best-known version of the ambitious claim was the observation, made in 1996, that no two countries with a McDonald's had ever gone to war with each other. It was contradicted within three years by the bombing of Belgrade, and several times since. When McDonald's withdrew from Russia in 2022 and its restaurants reopened under a Russian name, the direction of causation was plain: commerce follows politics rather than steering it.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — View B: Sameness Empties Places of Meaning

The opposing view holds that a shopping street is not merely a container for transactions. When every town centre offers the same shopfronts in the same order, places lose the distinctiveness that gives residents something to belong to and visitors a reason to come. Critics add that the choice on offer is narrower than it appears: shelves are fuller than ever, yet the range of genuinely different things — different tastes, different ways of making, different ways of trading — steadily contracts behind a surface of variety.

Philosophical concept: McDonaldization, developing Weber: systems optimised for efficiency, calculability, predictability and control eventually produce "the irrationality of rationality," defeating the human purposes they were built to serve. The idea of the non-place names the result — a space with no identity, no history and no relations, which produces solitary transactions rather than belonging. Two further strands deepen the critique: pseudo-individualisation, the appearance of choice masking real uniformity, and the capability approach, on which freedom is measured by the range of genuinely different lives available rather than by the number of items on a shelf.

Example: The New Economics Foundation's Clone Town Britain survey counted fifty shops on each of 103 British high streets and classified 41 per cent of them as "clone towns" — centres whose independent character had been replaced by the same national and global chains — with only a third retaining a distinctive mix; in London the figure rose to 48 per cent. Exeter scored 6.9 out of 60 for diversity while Hebden Bridge scored 48.6, which is the whole argument in two numbers: towns three hundred miles apart had become interchangeable, and the towns that resisted were the ones people travelled to see.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — Your Position: Function Versus Meaning

These positions can be reconciled by asking not how similar shops have become, but what kind of value is at stake in each case. Where a product is valued for performing a function — a medicine, a charger, a screwdriver — variation is simply a defect, and we should want it engineered away as thoroughly as possible. Where the value lies in meaning — the square where a town gathers, the dish that carries a region's history, the bookshop that forms a reader's taste — uniformity destroys the very thing being bought. The reasonable response, then, is not resistance to standardisation in general but deliberate protection of the narrow class of goods and places whose worth cannot survive it.

Philosophical concept: A distinction between instrumental goods, valued for what they produce, and constitutive goods, which are part of what makes a life or a community worth having. Two ideas explain why the second class deserves protection: experiments in living and value pluralism hold that diversity has epistemic value, because different ways of doing things are how a society discovers better ones, so uniformity forecloses discoveries not yet made. The notion of embeddedness adds the social half — commerce once sat inside relationships of acquaintance and obligation, and lifting it out of them is precisely what makes a street feel like nowhere. The distinction is useful in an exam because it tells you what to defend and what to let go, which keeps the essay from collapsing into nostalgia.

Example: France's Lang Law of 1981 forbids retailers from discounting a new book more than five per cent below the publisher's price, and was extended to e-books in 2011 — a deliberate decision to treat books as a cultural good rather than a purely commercial one, while leaving French consumers entirely free to buy standardised global products everywhere else. According to the Centre National du Livre, 129 independent bookshops opened in France in 2024 against 72 closures, a net gain in a trade that has contracted sharply in countries without such protection. It is targeted protection where meaning is at stake, combined with open competition where it is not.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between reliability and distinctiveness, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that the convergence of shops and products is a genuine gain wherever we buy things for what they do, and a real loss wherever we buy them for what they mean, so the task is not to resist uniformity but to decide, deliberately, where it should not be allowed to reach.

6. Handling the Counter-Argument (an optional Band 8 move)

The strongest objection to the negative view is that nobody is forced into a chain store: if shoppers keep choosing the same shops, then sameness is simply what people want, and the complaint is aesthetic snobbery dressed up as principle. The sharpest reply is that this is a collective action problem — each shopper rationally choosing the cheaper and more reliable option produces, in aggregate, a high street that few of those same shoppers would have voted for. Individual preference and collective outcome come apart, which is exactly why cities legislate: Amsterdam banned new tourist-oriented shops in its centre in 2017, a restriction upheld by the highest Dutch court in 2020. Raising an objection and answering it in two or three sentences is one of the clearest signals of a Band 8 Task Response — but only attempt it if you can do it briefly.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Standardisation as reliability — a recognisable brand is a portable promise, letting strangers transact without verifying anything themselves.
  • The egalitarian argument — uniform products break the link between the quality of what you can buy and where you happen to live (a Rawlsian test: judge by the worst-off).
  • Doux commerce — the classical claim that trade softens manners and makes war unprofitable (Montesquieu; Kant).
  • Contact versus parallel consumption — meeting people reduces prejudice, whereas owning the same objects only creates the conditions in which meeting might happen.
  • Thin versus thick common ground — recognising the same brands is not the same as understanding how another society reasons; the first does not accumulate into the second by itself.
  • The irrationality of rationality — systems optimised for efficiency, predictability and control end by defeating the purposes they were built to serve (Weber; Ritzer's McDonaldization).
  • Non-places — spaces with no identity, history or relations, which produce transactions rather than belonging (Augé).
  • Pseudo-individualisation — the appearance of variety concealing real uniformity; twenty flavours, one formula (Adorno).
  • Choice versus freedom — freedom is the range of genuinely different lives available, not the number of items on a shelf (Sen).
  • Experiments in living / value pluralism — diversity deserves protection because different ways of doing things are how a society finds better ones (Mill; Berlin).
  • Embeddedness — commerce once sat inside relationships of acquaintance and obligation, and disembedding it is what makes a street feel anonymous (Polanyi).
  • Function versus meaning — uniformity is a virtue in goods valued for what they do, and a loss in goods and places valued for what they mean.
  • Collective action problem — rational individual choices aggregating into an outcome that nobody actually chose.

Reminder: do not name philosophers in the exam. The marks are for the reasoning, not the citation — "a street that could be anywhere belongs to no one" scores; "as Marc Augé argues" does not, and unattributed ideas read as your own thinking.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format, in roughly 300 words. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: In most countries, shops and products are becoming increasingly similar. Some people think this has positive effects, while others think it has negative effects. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Whether in Shanghai or Santiago, shoppers increasingly meet the same chains selling the same goods. Some welcome this convergence as a guarantee of quality; others see the slow erasure of local character. This essay examines both before arguing that uniformity serves goods valued for what they do, but damages those valued for what they mean.

The first view rests on dependability: a recognisable brand is a portable promise, telling the buyer what it will do and whom to blame if it fails, so risk leaves ordinary purchases. The gain is quietly egalitarian: a provincial shopper can now buy what the capital buys. The World Health Organization estimates that one in ten medical products in poorer countries is substandard or falsified — markets where nobody answers for what they sell.

The opposing view holds that a shopping street is more than a container for transactions. When every centre offers the same shopfronts in the same order, places lose the distinctiveness that gives residents something to belong to, and real choice narrows behind a surface of variety. Clone Town Britain found 41 per cent of high streets interchangeable; the towns that resisted were the ones people travelled to see.

The two can be reconciled by asking what kind of value is at stake. Where a product performs a function, such as a medicine or a charger, variation is simply a defect. Where value lies in meaning, as in a town square or a bookshop that shapes a reader's taste, uniformity destroys what is bought. France caps discounts on new books for this reason, and 129 independent bookshops opened there in 2024 against 72 closures.

In conclusion, similarity is a gain where we buy things for what they do and a loss where we buy them for what they mean; the task is not to resist uniformity but to decide where it must not reach.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • convergence — the process by which separate things become more and more alike; the exam-ready noun for "shops and products becoming similar."
  • the erasure of local character — the gradual disappearance of what made a place distinctive; a formal alternative to "losing culture."
  • uniformity — the state of being the same everywhere; the key abstract noun of this topic, more precise than "sameness."
  • dependability — the quality of being reliable and behaving as expected every time.
  • a portable promise — an image for a brand: a guarantee the buyer can carry into any country or shop.
  • to hold someone responsible — to be able to identify and blame the party at fault if something goes wrong.
  • risk is removed from ordinary purchases — everyday buying stops being a gamble because the outcome is known in advance.
  • quietly egalitarian — producing greater equality without being designed or advertised as doing so.
  • substandard or falsified — below the required quality, or deliberately faked; the WHO's own terms for unsafe medicines.
  • a container for transactions — a place treated only as somewhere to buy and sell, with no other human value.
  • distinctiveness — the quality of being noticeably different from others; what a "clone town" has lost.
  • something to belong to — a place or community that gives people an identity and a sense of membership.
  • a surface of variety — an appearance of choice that conceals real sameness underneath (pseudo-individualisation in plain English).
  • interchangeable — so similar that one could replace the other without anyone noticing.
  • variation is simply a defect — for functional goods, being different is a fault rather than a virtue.
  • to be engineered away — to be deliberately eliminated through better design and manufacturing.
  • what a product does versus what it means — the essay's named principle: function (instrumental value) against meaning (constitutive value).
  • to decide deliberately where it should not reach — to choose consciously which goods and places to protect from standardisation.
