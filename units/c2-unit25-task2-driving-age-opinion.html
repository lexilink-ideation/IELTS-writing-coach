C2 Task 2

Unit 25: Is Raising the Driving Age the Best Way to Improve Road Safety? — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Agree / Disagree, Attacking a Superlative, Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree / Disagree) essay, and "to what extent do you agree or disagree" is not the same as "discuss both views" — you must take a position and hold it from the first paragraph to the last. Crucially, the claim in this prompt is a superlative: it does not say that raising the age helps road safety, but that it is the best way to improve it. That single word is your entire opening move. You can accept that a minimum age is justified and still disagree sharply that raising it is the best strategy — indeed, that concession-plus-rejection is the strongest thesis available.

A second warning specific to this question: "raising the age" is relative to a starting point that varies by country. Raising it from 16 to 17 is a different proposal from raising it from 18 to 21, and each faces different objections. Naming the baseline you are discussing costs one clause in your introduction and is itself a Band 8 move, because clarifying an ambiguous term is exactly the judgement examiners reward.

A third warning: this is a question about a policy, not about teenagers. "Young people are reckless" is a stereotype, is contested, and even where true wins no marks on its own. Every point must attach to the proposal itself — what it would actually remove from the roads, what it would cost, and whether another measure would do the job better.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): paraphrase the question, name the baseline age you are discussing, and state your position. Paragraph 2 (The case for): the youngest drivers genuinely are the most dangerous, and since driving endangers others, society is entitled to regulate it, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (The case against): age is only a proxy for the real causes, and "best" is a superlative that fails against cheaper, better-aimed measures, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Your position): road safety is a systems problem, so a sensible minimum age is one layer among many, not the single lever, one specific example. Conclusion: restate the tension and give a confident, qualified final opinion.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • Attack the superlative, not the aim. "Raising the age is the best way" is not the same claim as "raising the age helps." Your thesis should be: the aim is right, this is not the best means.
  • Name your reasoning in plain English (the proxy problem, the novice effect, one layer not the lever) — naming the logic makes the structure visible and supports both coherence and task response.
  • Concede a limit. "The data shows the youngest are riskiest, though not that age itself is the cause" — naming what your evidence cannot show is the judgement that separates Band 7 from Band 8.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, figures) beats breadth.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 270-310 words total, and reserve three minutes at the end to check articles, plurals and subject-verb agreement.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question, name the baseline age you are discussing, and state your position.
  • Body Paragraph 1 (The case for) — the youngest drivers genuinely are the most dangerous, and since driving endangers others, society is entitled to regulate it.
  • Body Paragraph 2 (The case against) — age is only a proxy for the real causes, and "best" is a superlative that fails against cheaper, better-aimed measures.
  • Body Paragraph 3 (Your position) — road safety is a systems problem, so a sensible minimum age is one layer among many, not the single lever.
  • Conclusion — restate the tension and give a confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, name the baseline age you are discussing, and put your opinion here — not in the conclusion. Concede that regulating young drivers is reasonable, then deny that raising the age is the best route to safety.

Example paraphrase: "Road deaths remain among the most preventable of modern tragedies, and since the drivers most often involved are the youngest, raising the legal driving age has an obvious intuitive appeal. Yet a measure can reduce a problem without being the best answer to it. This essay accepts that a minimum age is justified, because driving endangers strangers as well as the driver; but it argues that raising that age is not the best way to improve road safety, because age is merely a stand-in for the real causes — inexperience, impulsiveness and impairment — and because far more precise measures reach those causes at a fraction of the social cost."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For: The Youngest Drivers Really Are the Most Dangerous

The strongest version of the pro-raising argument does not rest on prejudice against the young; it rests on data and on a principle. The data is that crash risk is steepest at the very start of driving life and among the very youngest drivers. The principle is that driving is not a private act: a car threatens pedestrians, passengers and other motorists who never accepted the risk. Because the conduct is other-regarding, society is entitled to set conditions on it, and an age threshold is one such condition. If the youngest cohort is measurably the most lethal, removing it from the road for a year or two will, other things being equal, save lives.

Philosophical concept: Mill's harm principle is usually quoted to resist state control, but here it licenses it: liberty may be restricted to prevent harm to others, and driving is a textbook case of risk imposed on non-consenting third parties, so it is not sheltered by "it's my choice." Behind the data sits a developmental claim — the brain's prefrontal cortex, which governs impulse control and risk appraisal, is commonly held to keep maturing into the mid-twenties — which makes youth a plausible marker of the impulsiveness that causes crashes.

Example: In the United States, where the driving age is unusually low, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety reports that the fatal crash rate per mile driven for sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds is roughly three times that of drivers aged twenty and over. New Jersey, which sets its full minimum at seventeen — the highest in the country — combined with a graduated system, has consistently recorded lower teenage crash rates than states that license at sixteen. On its face, later licensing is associated with fewer young-driver deaths.

Developing the point — but note the limit. State the limit, because doing so earns credit for judgement. This evidence shows that the youngest drivers are the riskiest; it does not show that their age is the cause rather than their inexperience. New Jersey's teenagers also drive under a graduated licence, so the gain cannot be credited to the number seventeen alone. The data proves that something about new, young drivers is dangerous — not that a later birthday is the cure.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case Against: Age Is a Proxy, and "Best" Is a Superlative

The opposing view makes two moves, one about causation and one about proportion. The first: age does not cause crashes; it correlates with the things that do — inexperience, a taste for risk, and driving while impaired by alcohol, speed or a phone. The decisive fact is the novice effect: crash risk falls steeply during the first year or two behind the wheel whatever age one starts, so a freshly licensed twenty-one-year-old carries much of the risk we blame on the eighteen-year-old. Raising the age therefore does not remove the dangerous phase; it postpones it onto a slightly older set of beginners. To attack the cause through the proxy is a single-cause fallacy — treating a crash, which is the product of many aligned failures, as if it had one dial marked "age."

The second move is about proportion and fairness. A blanket rise restricts an entire age group for the conduct of a reckless minority within it: it judges people by the category they fall into, their birth year, rather than by anything they have actually done. It also falls hardest on those with the fewest alternatives — the rural young, and low-income young people who must drive to reach work, study or a relative they care for. And then there is the word "best." Even granting that raising the age helps, "best" is a comparative claim, and it collapses the moment a cheaper, more targeted measure delivers comparable safety.

Philosophical concept: Three ideas do the work. Correlation is not causation: age is a stand-in for inexperience, and policy aimed at a stand-in misses its target. The Swiss cheese model of accidents (James Reason) holds that disasters happen when many separate weaknesses line up, so safety comes from layered defences, never from one lever. And Kant's formula of humanity resists using a whole cohort merely as a means to an aggregate statistic: a just rule addresses conduct, not category. A Rawlsian test then asks who bears the cost, and finds it is the young people with the fewest options, not the privileged who can afford other transport.

Example: Graduated Driver Licensing is the standing rebuttal. New Zealand introduced a comprehensive graduated system in 1987, and many jurisdictions that followed — across Australia, Canada and the United States — have been associated with substantial falls in young-driver crashes, with reductions commonly reported in the range of a fifth to two-fifths. Graduated licensing works precisely because it targets the cause rather than the proxy: it lets novices accumulate experience under lower-risk conditions — supervised practice, night-time and passenger limits, zero-alcohol rules — instead of simply forbidding a birthday. Enforcement tells the same story: after New South Wales introduced random breath testing in 1982, road fatalities in the state fell by roughly a fifth within the first year.

Developing the point — the comparative test. Name the limit again: graduated licensing is not costless, and it still lets inexperienced people drive, so it too reduces rather than eliminates risk. But that is precisely the comparative point the word "best" forces. The right question is not whether raising the age does something, but whether it does more, per unit of liberty lost, than the alternatives. Measured that way, a blunt ban on a whole cohort compares poorly with a scalpel aimed at inexperience, drink and speed.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — The Position: One Layer, Not the Lever

The disagreement survives because the word "best" smuggles in a single-lever picture of safety, as if there were one master control to turn. There is not. Road safety is a systems outcome, produced by many defences acting together: driver training and graduated experience, the enforcement of drink, speed and phone laws, the engineering of roads and junctions, the crash-worthiness of vehicles, and, yes, a sensible minimum age. Seen this way, the reasonable position is not to reject an age limit and not to crown it either, but to place it correctly: a minimum age is one useful layer among many, while calling its raising the "best way" mistakes a single slice for the whole cheese. The examiner-facing formulation is short — build the system, do not just raise the number.

Philosophical concept: the principle of the least restrictive means settles the design: where a targeted measure achieves the goal, the blanket one cannot be justified, because the extra liberty it costs buys no extra safety. Systems thinking — the philosophy behind Sweden's Vision Zero — reframes safety as a property to be engineered across the whole system rather than extracted by blaming or banning one class of user.

Example: Sweden is the standing demonstration. Its parliament adopted Vision Zero in 1997, built on the premise that no death in traffic is acceptable and that responsibility lies chiefly with those who design the system, not only with those who use it. Through lower urban speed limits, separated lanes, safer junctions, seat-belt and sobriety enforcement and safer vehicles, Sweden roughly halved its road deaths over the following two decades and now has among the lowest road-fatality rates per capita in the world — all while keeping its driving age at eighteen. It achieved what the raise-the-age proposal wants, and did it by rebuilding the system rather than by moving the number.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between saving lives and restricting a whole cohort, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for instance, that a minimum driving age is justified but raising it is not the best way to improve road safety, because the gains it promises are delivered more precisely, more fairly and more cheaply by graduated licensing, enforcement and better road design. The useful question for a government is therefore not whether young drivers are risky, which no one denies, but whether the largest safety gains come from a later birthday or from a better system.

6. Handling the Counter-Argument (an optional Band 8 move)

The strongest objection to the position above is that every alternative still lets inexperienced people drive, whereas raising the age is the one measure that removes the highest-risk group outright — a certain gain, where graduated licensing only lowers a probability. If lives are the currency, a guaranteed removal may look superior to a mere reduction.

The reply is to concede the case and then confine it. It is true that raising the age removes that particular cohort with certainty. But it does not remove the risk — it relocates it onto the next cohort of novices, who will be just as inexperienced at twenty-one as at eighteen; the certainty is of a smaller kind than it first appears. Meanwhile the cost is genuinely certain: mobility denied to millions of responsible young people, concentrated on those who can least absorb it. A guaranteed large cost to buy a partial, displaced gain is a poor exchange — which is why the sounder policy pairs a modest minimum age with graduated licensing, rather than reaching, again and again, for a higher number. Raising a strong objection and answering it in two or three sentences is one of the clearest signals of Band 8 Task Response — but only attempt it if you can be brief.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • The harm principle — driving imposes risk on non-consenting third parties, so it is other-regarding conduct that society may legitimately regulate; this justifies having a minimum age, not raising it indefinitely (Mill).
  • Correlation is not causation / the single-cause fallacy — age correlates with crashes, but the causes are inexperience, risk-taking and impairment; a policy aimed at the proxy misses the target.
  • The novice effect — crash risk falls steeply in the first year or two of driving whatever the starting age, so raising the age relocates the dangerous phase rather than removing it.
  • The Swiss cheese model / systems thinking — accidents happen when many separate weaknesses align, so safety comes from layered defences, never from one lever (James Reason).
  • Proportionality / the least restrictive means — where a targeted measure achieves the goal, a blanket restriction cannot be justified, because the extra liberty it costs buys no extra safety.
  • Treating persons as ends — a blanket age rise uses a whole cohort merely as a means to an aggregate statistic, judging people by category (birth year) rather than by conduct (Kant).
  • Judge by the worst-off — the cost falls hardest on rural and low-income young people who depend on driving and have no alternative transport (Rawls).
  • The superlative test — "best way" is a comparative claim; it fails the moment any cheaper, better-aimed measure delivers comparable safety.
  • Vision Zero — reframing road safety as a property to be engineered across the whole system, rather than extracted by banning one class of road user.

Reminder: do not name philosophers in the exam. The marks are for the reasoning, not the citation — "a crash is the product of many aligned failures, not one dial marked age" scores; "as Kant argued" does not.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, in roughly 300 words. Notice that the writer's position is visible in every paragraph — "I accept", "I accept this much", "decisive", "in my view", "I therefore conclude" — so the examiner never has to guess whose view is being presented. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box, using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: Some people think that raising the legal minimum driving age is the best way to improve road safety. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Road deaths remain among the most preventable tragedies of modern life, and since the youngest drivers are involved most often, raising the legal driving age has an obvious appeal. I accept that a minimum age is justified, but I will argue that raising it is not the best way to improve road safety, since age is only a stand-in for the real causes of crashes.

The case for raising the age rests on data and a principle. Crash risk is steepest among the very youngest drivers, and because driving endangers strangers as well as the driver, society is entitled to regulate it. In the United States, the fatal crash rate for sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds is roughly three times that of drivers aged twenty and over. I accept this much: the youngest drivers genuinely are the riskiest.

Two objections nevertheless defeat the claim that raising the age is best. The first is the novice effect: crash risk falls steeply during a driver's first year or two whatever the starting age, so raising it simply relocates the dangerous phase rather than removing it. The second is decisive: "best" is a comparative claim, and it collapses once a cheaper, better-aimed measure performs as well. New Zealand's graduated licensing system has cut young-driver crashes by targeting inexperience directly rather than restricting an entire age cohort.

In my view the dispute survives only because "best" smuggles in the idea of a single master lever, when road safety is a systems outcome produced by many defences together — training, enforcement, road design and vehicle safety, alongside a sensible minimum age. Sweden's Vision Zero policy roughly halved road deaths over two decades this way, while keeping its driving age at eighteen throughout.

I therefore conclude that a minimum driving age is justified, but that raising it is not the best way to improve road safety, since the same gains are delivered more precisely by graduated licensing, enforcement and better road design.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • a stand-in for — a substitute that represents something else without being identical to it; used to describe how age represents, but is not itself, the real cause of a crash.
  • the fatal crash rate — the number of fatal crashes measured against distance driven or population, used to compare the risk carried by different groups of drivers.
  • I accept this much — a phrase that concedes part of an opposing argument before limiting exactly how far that concession goes.
  • the novice effect — the finding that crash risk falls sharply during a driver's first year or two regardless of age; the named principle that undercuts the case for raising the age.
  • relocates ... rather than removing — shifts a problem onto a different group instead of eliminating it; the core objection to postponing risk by raising an age threshold.
  • a comparative claim — a claim that ranks one option against its alternatives, so it fails the moment a better option exists; the reason the word "best" is vulnerable to attack.
  • targeting X directly — aiming a measure precisely at the actual cause of a problem, rather than at a rough proxy for it.
  • restricting an entire age cohort — imposing a rule on everyone born within a certain range of years, rather than on the individuals whose conduct is actually the problem.
  • smuggles in — quietly introduces an assumption without stating or defending it; used here to describe the hidden claim inside the word "best."
  • a single master lever — the mistaken idea that one control alone can solve a complex problem; the image the essay uses to reject the superlative claim.
  • a systems outcome — a result produced by many interacting parts working together, rather than by any one factor in isolation.
  • acting together — functioning jointly rather than in isolation; used to describe how several road-safety measures combine to produce an effect.
  • delivered more precisely — achieved with less waste and less collateral cost than a blunter alternative.
  • I therefore conclude — a formal phrase that signals the final, considered judgement of the essay.

6 Self-Review Checklist

  • My introduction paraphrases the prompt AND states my own position explicitly (not saved for the conclusion).
  • I named the baseline age I am discussing (e.g. 16→17 vs 18→21) in the introduction.
  • My thesis attacks the superlative ("best way"), not the aim — I accept a minimum age is justified but reject that raising it is the best strategy.
  • I gave the case FOR raising the age a full, sympathetic paragraph with a named principle (the harm principle) and one specific example.
  • I gave the case AGAINST a full paragraph with a named principle (the novice effect, or the proxy problem) and one specific example.
  • My position paragraph offers a reasoned resolution ("one layer, not the lever") with an example.
  • I kept the essay about the POLICY — what it removes, what it costs, whether another measure does better — rather than a generic claim that young people are reckless.
  • I conceded at least one limit of my own evidence (e.g. that the data shows the youngest are riskiest, not that age itself is the cause).
  • My conclusion is short, uses a linking word, and introduces no new examples.
