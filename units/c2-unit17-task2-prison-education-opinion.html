C2 Task 2

Unit 17: Prison or Better Education — A Philosophical Approach

Skill focus: Full Opinion Essay (Philosophical Reasoning)

1 Mini Lesson

This is an Opinion (Agree/Disagree) essay at its most sophisticated. Rather than simply agreeing or disagreeing, a Band 9 response weighs two competing views of how a society should best deal with crime — prison as a way to punish and contain wrongdoing versus education as a way to prevent it at the source — before landing on a qualified, partial-agreement position. Every paragraph still follows the same core discipline you have practiced throughout the curriculum: an introduction with a background statement and a clear thesis statement, body paragraphs that open with a topic sentence and restate your position, and a short conclusion that adds no new content.

Paragraph 1 (Introduction): background statement (paraphrase) + thesis statement (a qualified position). Paragraph 2 (For prison): punishment works — deterrence, incapacitation and justice, one specific example. Paragraph 3 (For education): prevention beats cure — crime springs from ignorance and disadvantage, one specific example. Paragraph 4 (Middle path): a principle that resolves the tension (prevention first, punishment as safeguard) + your reasoned position. Paragraph 5 (Conclusion): summary + confident final opinion, no new examples.

Four habits separate a Band 8-9 response from a Band 6-7 one at this level:

  • A partial-agreement thesis ("education is the more effective solution, but prison is still necessary — for this specific reason") scores better than a flat yes/no — it shows the nuance examiners look for at Band 7+.
  • Name a principle (deterrence, prevention over punishment, prevention-first) — even in plain English, without jargon — to give your reasoning visible structure.
  • Use one well-developed real-world example per body paragraph rather than several vague ones — specificity (names, places, figures) beats breadth.
  • Keep the counter-productivity point analytical, never one-sided: say prison can entrench offending, not that it never works — and always tie the philosophy back to the exact question.

Aim for four to five paragraphs and 260-300 words total.

2 Essay Outline

Essay Structure at a Glance

  • Introduction — paraphrase the question + state a partial-agreement thesis.
  • Body Paragraph 1 — the case FOR prison (punishment works: deterrence, incapacitation and justice).
  • Body Paragraph 2 — the case FOR education (prevention beats cure: crime springs from ignorance and disadvantage).
  • Body Paragraph 3 — a middle path (prevention as primary, punishment as a necessary safeguard) + your reasoned position.
  • Conclusion — summarize + confident, qualified final opinion.

1. Introduction

Paraphrase the prompt, then signal that you will weigh two competing views of how a society should best deal with crime — prison as a way to punish and contain wrongdoing versus education as a way to prevent it at the source — before landing on a qualified position. Avoid a flat, one-sided thesis; IELTS examiners reward a nuanced stance in agree/disagree essays as much as a decisive one.

Example paraphrase: "It is widely held that imprisonment is the most effective way to control crime, though others argue that investing in better education would do more to reduce it. This essay will examine why prison remains a necessary response to serious wrongdoing, why education is nonetheless the more powerful long-term solution because it addresses the causes of crime rather than its symptoms, and will argue that education deserves priority while prison is retained as a safeguard."

2. Body Paragraph 1 — The Case For Prison: Punishment Works

There is a strong case that prison is an effective solution because it does three things at once. It deters: the threat of losing one's liberty raises the "cost" of crime and discourages would-be offenders. It incapacitates: a person behind bars simply cannot commit further offences against the public. And it delivers justice: serious harm seems to demand a proportionate response, independent of any future benefit. On this view, prison protects society now, in a way that a classroom cannot.

Philosophical concept: The deterrence-and-desert view combines two traditions. Utilitarian deterrence (Bentham, and Beccaria's argument that the certainty of punishment matters more than its severity) treats prison as justified by the future crime it prevents. Retributive justice (Kant, for whom punishment is deserved as a matter of justice, not merely as a tool) holds that wrongdoing must be answered in proportion to its gravity.

Example: Singapore pairs strict sentencing and highly certain enforcement with consistently low rates of violent crime, illustrating the deterrence-and-incapacitation case that firm, reliable punishment can keep public offending rare — the strongest practical argument for prison as an effective solution.

3. Body Paragraph 2 — The Case For Education: Prevention Beats Cure

Yet prison is largely reactive: it acts only after a crime has been committed and does little about why crime happens in the first place. Two ideas support the rival claim that education is the more effective solution. First, much wrongdoing grows from ignorance, poverty and lack of opportunity — conditions that schooling can change but a cell cannot. Second, prison can be actively counter-productive, teaching criminal habits and stigmatising released people so they reoffend. If the goal is less crime overall, addressing the causes may achieve more than punishing the results.

Philosophical concept: Two strands combine here. The prevention tradition (Beccaria's maxim that "it is better to prevent crimes than to punish them," the Socratic view in Plato that wrongdoing stems from ignorance, and Rousseau's claim that people are corrupted by unequal conditions rather than born bad) holds that education attacks crime at its root. The counter-productivity critique (Foucault's argument in Discipline and Punish that prisons tend to manufacture repeat "delinquents") holds that punitive institutions can entrench the very behaviour they are meant to stop. A related idea sharpens both: the situationist thesis — that systems and situations, not just fixed character, push ordinary people toward wrongdoing ("bad barrels, not bad apples") — implies a society reduces crime best by building formative environments rather than punitive ones. (Use this as a concept, not proof: reference the idea of situational influence rather than citing the contested Stanford experiment as evidence.)

Example: Norway's education- and rehabilitation-focused prisons, such as Halden, report reoffending rates of around 20 per cent, far below the 50-70 per cent common in more punitive systems — evidence that teaching skills and offering a future does more to stop future crime than confinement alone, and that where prison works best it works by educating.

4. Body Paragraph 3 — A Middle Path: Prevention First, Punishment as Safeguard

The two positions can be reconciled by distinguishing what each is good for. Prison and education are not rival answers to the same question but tools for different stages of the problem: education is the primary, long-term solution because it reduces the number of people who ever turn to crime, while prison is a necessary safeguard for the serious offences that prevention fails to stop and for which justice and public safety still require a response. A rational society therefore invests first in prevention and reserves imprisonment for where it is genuinely needed, rather than treating cells as the default cure.

Example: The Nordic model — heavy public investment in schooling and social support combined with small, rehabilitative prison systems — produces both low crime and low reoffending, showing that prevention and punishment work best as partners, with education carrying the main load and prison acting as a limited backstop rather than the frontline solution.

5. Conclusion

Restate the tension between prison as a way to punish and contain crime and education as a way to prevent it, then give a brief, confident, qualified opinion — for example, that while prison remains indispensable for serious wrongdoing, better education is the more effective solution overall because it tackles the roots of crime, so societies should treat it as the priority and prison as the safeguard rather than the reverse.

Key Philosophical Concepts — Quick Reference

  • Deterrence and incapacitation — prison reduces crime by raising its "cost" and by removing offenders from society (Bentham; Beccaria on the certainty of punishment).
  • Retributive justice — serious wrongdoing deserves a proportionate punishment as a matter of justice, not only as a deterrent (Kant).
  • Prevention over punishment — crime springs largely from ignorance and disadvantage, which education can change and prison cannot (Beccaria, Socrates/Plato, Rousseau).
  • The counter-productivity critique — punitive prisons can manufacture repeat offenders rather than reduce crime (Foucault; high recidivism).
  • The situationist thesis — systems and situations, not just fixed character, shape whether people offend, so better formative environments (education) prevent crime ("bad barrels, not bad apples").
  • Prevention first, punishment as safeguard — education and prison are complementary, with prevention as the primary solution and imprisonment a limited backstop.

3 Band 9 Sample Essay

This is a complete Band 9 worked answer to the exact question below, written in the background-statement-plus-thesis / topic-sentence / restated-opinion format. Read it closely, then write your own response to the same question in the Writing Box — using different real-world examples than the ones used here.

Question: In most countries, prison is an effective solution for the problem of crime. Some people think a more effective solution is to provide a better education. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

It is widely held that imprisonment is the most effective way to control crime, though others contend that investing in better education would achieve more. In my view, while prison remains indispensable for serious wrongdoing, better education is the more effective solution overall, because it addresses crime's causes rather than merely its symptoms.

There is a compelling case that prison works, for it deters, incapacitates and delivers justice at once. The threat of lost liberty raises the cost of offending, those confined cannot harm the public, and grave harm arguably demands a proportionate response. Singapore, for instance, pairs strict sentencing with certain enforcement and records consistently low rates of violent crime, illustrating that firm, reliable punishment can keep offending rare.

Prison, however, is largely reactive: it acts only after a crime has been committed and does little about why crime occurs. Much wrongdoing grows from ignorance, poverty and lack of opportunity — conditions schooling can change but a cell cannot — and punitive institutions can even entrench criminal habits. Norway's rehabilitation-focused prisons, such as Halden, report reoffending rates of around 20 per cent, far below the 50 to 70 per cent common in harsher systems, suggesting that teaching skills prevents future crime more effectively than confinement alone.

These positions can be reconciled: prison and education serve different stages of the problem. Education is the primary, long-term solution because it reduces the number who ever turn to crime, whereas prison is a necessary safeguard for the serious offences prevention fails to stop. The Nordic model, which combines investment in schooling with small, rehabilitative prisons, yields both low crime and low reoffending, showing that the two work best as partners.

In conclusion, although prison is indispensable for containing serious wrongdoing, better education is the more effective solution overall, so societies should treat prevention as the priority and imprisonment as the safeguard rather than the reverse.

4 Key Vocabulary from the Sample Essay

English-to-English definitions of useful words and phrases from the Band 9 essay above — target vocabulary you can reuse in your own response.

  • indispensable — absolutely necessary; impossible to manage without.
  • to control crime — to keep the amount of crime down and prevent it from spreading.
  • to contend (that) — to argue or claim something is true, especially in a debate.
  • to deter — to discourage someone from doing something by making the consequences seem too costly or risky.
  • to incapacitate — to make someone unable to act; here, to remove offenders from society so they cannot commit further crimes.
  • a proportionate response — a reaction whose severity matches the seriousness of the offence — no harsher and no softer than deserved.
  • certain enforcement — the reliable, consistent application of the law, so that offenders are highly likely to be caught and punished.
  • reactive — responding only after something has already happened, rather than acting in advance to prevent it (opposite: proactive).
  • to entrench (a habit) — to fix something so firmly in place that it becomes very hard to change or remove.
  • rehabilitation-focused — designed mainly to reform offenders and prepare them to return to normal life, rather than only to punish them.
  • reoffending / recidivism — the tendency of a released offender to commit further crimes.
  • confinement — the state of being kept in prison or otherwise physically restricted.
  • to reconcile (two positions) — to find a way of making two opposing views fit together consistently.
  • a safeguard — a measure kept in place to protect against a specific danger when other measures fail.
  • a backstop — a last line of defence used only when the primary approach does not work.
  • to treat X as the priority — to regard X as the most important thing and deal with it first.
